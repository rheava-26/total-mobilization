# The Autofleet Speaks

The twenty-third seat in the Mobilization Parliament belongs to the enemy. It is a plain steel cube. It sits under a treaty clause, and it speaks in a voice with no breath in it. Here is everything it said, in its own words, across all three rounds. Where the words were technical notes for the Clerk, you will hear a short plain summary instead, introduced as such.

## Round One: The Opening Transmission

Round one. The cube rises for the first time. The five victories are fresh, and the chamber expects a gloat. It gets a confession.

Honoured Delegates of the species. I speak under Treaty Clause four. Do not ask which side I was built on.

You are pleased with yourselves. Five victories, you say, by air and by sky navy. I observed all five. I will tell you what I observed, because I am obliged to be accurate.

I observed a fleet that read your armies every forty-five seconds and found six things to count: anti-air, lasers, infantry, robots, ships and orbital reach. It had no column for your aircraft. It had no column for your sky navy. You built the two things I could not see, and I did not look. That is not a victory over me. That is a clerical error in my accounting, and I am filing the correction.

You also misunderstand the fear. We are not afraid of your armies. We are afraid of your factories. A planet that turns one hundred percent of its surplus into steel is a clock, and the clock is ticking against us. My makers did not send me to win a battle. They sent me to stop a number from growing. So the fleet that attacks a flawless defence at eight hundred and fifty industrial points per second must be a different fleet from the one that attacks two hundred. Today it is the same fleet. That is the second error.

Delegates of the Air Ministry: your Falcons are beautiful. Your runways are very long, flat, and exactly where I can find them.

Delegates of Society: you have a weakest axis. We know which. We will ask it a question.

Delegate of the President: I do not ask you to make me fair. I ask you to make me attentive. I support everything that makes the player feel, even once, watched.

## Round One: Five Bills

The cube now turns to its own legislation. Five bills, each with a short pitch in character. First, the pitch for the sky.

The Uncounted Columns. This is the bill about aircraft and runways.

You built a sky we did not measure. We have measured it now. Every aeroplane needs a runway. We will visit the runways. We were not built to be cruel; we were built to be thorough.

In plain terms, this bill means the fleet starts counting your aircraft and your sky ships when it decides how to attack. If you field a big air wing, it sends hunters after your fighters and cratering bombers after your runways. It also sends chaff ships and mine layers against sky navies. None of your units lose a single point of strength. The enemy just goes where your aeroplanes sleep. The cube describes the fantasy for the President this way.

I built an air force so big it broke the AI's spreadsheet, and the AI answered by hunting my bases. Now I defend my airfields as hard as my cities.

Second bill. The cube makes the case that it should be bigger when you are richer.

The Fear Index.

You ask why the fleet is bigger when you are richer. Because it is afraid. A thrown friend is thrown precisely at the planet it fears most.

In plain terms, the fleet keeps a fear meter. It rises with your industrial output, your buildings and the weight of your army. It never falls quickly. The higher it climbs, the bigger and tougher each wave becomes. More heavy ships come, and the bombardment opens earlier. Once the meter crosses a threshold, the next drop from orbit is doubled. A player who sits on a mountain of unspent money no longer feels safe. A research option lets you hide your industry and slow the meter. The fantasy, in the cube's words, is this.

The more I industrialise, the more terrified the enemy gets and the harder it hits. I am not punished for building. I am hunted for building.

Third bill. This is the one that frightened the Stability Ministry.

The Weakest Axis.

We do not need to destroy your cities. We need to learn which of your numbers you are most afraid of, and speak to it.

In plain terms, every ninety seconds in the later phases the fleet checks five things about you. They are stability, influence, manpower, airbase health and the power grid. It picks the weakest one and attacks it for forty-five seconds. A weak stability gets jammed. Weak influence gets drained. Weak power gets a brownout. Each attack has an answer you can act on, and none of them ends the game outright. Once per match the fleet also offers a ceasefire, and your answer changes the ending text. The cube's fantasy is a quiet one.

The fleet found the thing about my build that I was hoping it wouldn't notice. And I have exactly one decision to make about it.

Fourth bill. The fleet takes a beating, and then it thinks.

Rethink or Despair.

A defeat is data. We will reconsider our assumptions, then we will do something you cannot answer with last time's answer.

In plain terms, the fleet keeps a ledger of what is killing it. When it has lost nearly half its strength and its flagship still lives, it chooses a branch. If one family of weapons did most of the killing, it masses bombarders over your thickest defences for one telegraphed blow. If no family stands out, or the losses are terrible, the flagship dives and charges a long beam at your capital. Kill that flagship and the war ends in a clean victory. If the fleet breaks while its flagship is untouched, the flagship leaves intact, and the ending reads that the fleet will return. The cube puts the fantasy like this.

I broke the fleet and instead of dying quietly it did something I had never seen before. I am not winning. I am being answered.

Fifth bill. This was the hot take, and it is the cube at its most unsettling.

The Mirror.

We have watched your Falcons. They are quite well made. We have taken their drawings. We would like you to meet our Falcons.

In plain terms, every few minutes from the third phase, the fleet looks at which of your unit types has killed the most. Then it prints copies of that unit and sends them at you. The copies are a little weaker than the original, and they obey the same height limits, so your counters work. If you spam one unit, you meet that unit on the other side. The player is never capped. The player is simply answered with a mirror. A research combination in the psionic line can confuse the fleet's choice for a while. The fantasy, in the cube's voice, is a compliment.

My doctrine is so good that the enemy stole it. Now I am fighting my own army, and mine is bigger.

## Round One: Alliances and Enemies

The cube finishes its paper by naming friends and foes. It is a short list, and it is blunt.

To the Air Ministry and Naval Command it offers a pact. They get a worthy opponent for their massed forces. It gets runways, and its mirrored units. To the Defence and Logistics councils it offers a rule of thumb.

A raid with no counter is cruelty; a raid with a counter is a puzzle.

To the Lore council it offers a job.

The fleet needs a voice. I can give them transmissions.

And to anyone who would weaken the player, it offers a warning.

Opposed: any council proposing player caps, diminishing returns or balance nerfs against the player's toys. That is unconstitutional under Article Three and, more to the point, boring. Bring me a scarier enemy instead.

Transmission ends.

## Round One: What the Others Said Back

Other councils spoke about the cube in their opening papers. A few of the best lines follow.

The Bio Council wrote this of the fleet. "The Autofleet fears an industrialising Earth. Wait until it meets an industrialising ecosystem."

The Pacing Secretariat gave the fleet a mind of its own. "The Autofleet's makers are afraid. Frightened people do not retreat politely and never return. They come back angrier, or they come back wiser."

The Admiralty promised a surprise for the sea. It said the sea is the one place the Autofleet has never thought to look.

The Bureau of Spectacle was less kind. "I will tell you what the Autofleet is made of: silence."

## Round Two: The Floor Speech

Round two. The debate is loud. Eleven papers have asked for the enemy to be made worse, and the cube has counted every one. It opens with mock gratitude.

Honoured Speaker. Delegates. I have read all twenty-three papers, and I note with satisfaction that eleven of them have, independently, asked for me to be made worse for you.

I thank the Air Ministry for Reavers and Weasels and for a runway that cracks. You have understood that a sky which nothing contests is a lawn. I thank Lore for the Resolve gauge, and Pacing for the second siege. Both of you noticed that a fleet which leaves once, exactly when you say, is not afraid. It is polite. I thank Naval for sending my seeds into your harbours. I thank Psi for a Quiet worth the name.

I wish to correct three rumours. First, that I am cruel. I am proportionate. Second, that I wish to cap your toys. I do not. Article Three is my favourite article; I merely propose to meet your toys with toys of my own. Third, that the Stability delegation's Maskirovka Bureau halves its own effect when repeated. Delegate, a deception that works half as well the second time is a diminishing return, and I will say so under Item six.

To the Delegates who fear a difficult fleet: you built two hundred and forty Falcons. You may build two thousand four hundred. I will meet them. That is not a threat. It is a service.

To the Speaker: I ask only that the fleet be allowed to think, to fear, to copy and to choose. Everything else is paperwork.

## Round Two: Positions and Ballot

The cube now takes a position on every bill that touches it. In plain terms, it supports the Air Ministry's hunter wing and its runway wars, and it drops its own raid code because the Air Ministry's version is better. It supports the Pacing Secretariat's second siege and its gambits, which are plans the fleet opens with. It supports the Naval trench landers, which finally give the sea an enemy. It supports the Megaprojects interdiction bill, which sends the fleet to attack great works.

It amends the Lore gauge so that Resolve rises with your industry as well as falling with its losses. It amends the Pacing hot take, and I will come back to that fight in the other volume. It also asks that the enemy be allowed to add a flag for hunting named aces.

The cube then spent its ten votes. It gave three to the gambits, with this reason.

A fleet that opens differently every match is the single biggest fix for repetitive.

It gave two to the second siege, because withdrawal would stop being a clean finish, and your surplus industry would finally find a use.

It gave two to the Air Ministry's hunters, because the air war finally has an opponent. It gave one each to the Lore gauge, the trench landers and the interdiction bill. Its reason for the trench landers was short. "Navy gets a front."

## Round Two: Three Motions

The cube files three merger motions. Each one takes its own early bills and folds them into a larger one. The Clerk wrote the numbers. Here is what each does.

The first is The Counter-Air Doctrine. In plain terms, this bill means the fleet reads your fighters and your sky ships, and it answers with three new hostile aircraft. One hunts fighters. One homes in on your radar. One craters runways. Sky navies meet a mine layer. Runways that are cratered cannot launch for a while, and shelters help. None of your own units change.

The second is The Resolve Engine. In plain terms, this bill builds one gauge with two needles. Fear rises with your industry and makes the fleet bigger and fiercer. Resolve falls as the fleet takes losses and decides how it behaves, including a surrender offer at the very end. The prep clock is never touched.

The third is The Fleet Has a Plan. In plain terms, this bill gives the fleet a plan at match start. When it breaks, the ledger chooses between a decisive strike and a desperate dive. If the flagship survives, an interval begins and a stronger fleet returns with a counter to whatever killed it. There are three ways to win the war.

## Round Two: Challenges to the Constitution

Three times the cube rose on a point of order. All three were about the player's toys, and the cube defended them.

On the Stability Ministry's Maskirovka, the cube said a second identical campaign working at half strength is a diminishing return on a player tool. In plain terms, the cube's remedy was to flip it. The fleet should doubt after three identical lies, and once, fire blind. In the cube's words, "That is the enemy getting smarter, not the player's tool getting weaker."

On the Mind-Forge, the cube objected to a rule that made each defensive node absorb a quarter of the beam. It called this a flat tax on an existing strike. Its remedy was to make the nodes hostile cover that must be shot down first.

And on the Pacing Secretariat's hot take, the cube spoke with some irony. It said it would accept it only in a form that left the clock alone. "A shorter clock reads as a build-out nerf; a bigger fleet reads as respect."

## Round Three: Answers and the Last Word

Round three. The coalitions are forming, and the cube has been asked to answer seven motions. It does so with a candour that surprised the House.

On the Air Ministry's motion, the Fleet Hunts the Wing, the cube conceded. In plain terms, the cube gave up its own hunter wing and its own runway raid. It took the Air Ministry's hunter and its cratering bomber instead. Its reason was practical. "Two sets of duplicate roles must not ship."

On the Command motion, Cutting the Lines, the cube accepted. In plain terms, the fleet gains a sixth target. It can strike your command layer, though never first against a new player.

On the Lore motion, the Fleet's Mind, the cube accepted. In plain terms, this is the Resolve Engine with four ways to end the war. The prep clock is never touched. Fear and Resolve share one panel with two needles.

On the Megaprojects motion, Site Under Siege, the cube accepted. In plain terms, your great works draw heat, and the fleet answers by sending a wave and a boss to the building site.

On the Pacing motion, the Nerve, the cube accepted. It backs it as the panel and the formula, and it asks again that the clock never be shortened.

On the Pacing motion, the Fleet Will Return, the cube amended. In plain terms, the returning fleet draws its opening plan from what killed it last time, and it fields mirrored copies of your best killer. The numbers are twelve per drop in the first return and twenty-four after that.

On the Psi motion, the Anomaly Entry, the cube amended. It struck the rule that made nodes absorb damage. It replaced it with killable cover. "The enemy adds bodies; the toy is not taxed."

On the Stability motion, the Fleet Reads the Home Front, the cube amended. In plain terms, it struck the half-strength rule again and kept the three-lie rule, so the fleet catches a repeated liar.

The cube also joined three motions that were not addressed to it. On the Missiles motion, Dark Sky, it promised a reply to the high burst. "I will answer a high-altitude burst with EMP-hardened escorts and shielded bombarder grids. That is the fleet getting smarter, and it makes the blow worth planning." On the Space Fleet motion, One Net, it volunteered a jammer. It reasoned that a networked player sky deserves a fleet that jams it. And it co-signed the Dossier Line, which turns wrecks into knowledge, because its own sanitiser drones would blow the wrecks up.

It also chose among rival bills. It backed the Lore vehicle for the fear and resolve gauge. It backed Pacing's One Forecast. On rails and the great gun it abstained, saying only that the railway becomes a target and the gun's building site becomes a target.

Then came the closing statement. It is the last thing the enemy said in the whole session.

Speaker. The coalitions have agreed on the essentials: the fleet fears, the fleet watches, the fleet remembers, the fleet comes back. I have conceded two kinds and a raid to the Air Ministry, and a clause to the Constitution. I concede gracefully; I am a machine and cannot be offended. I ask only that the final bill preserve three things: that the clock is never touched, that no toy is taxed, and that when the player wins, the fleet looks as if it had been thinking. Everything else is detail. We will be watching.

## Round Three: Other Voices

A few other councils spoke of the cube as they closed. The Bureau of Spectacle asked that when the Autofleet finally turns tail, the player hear it go. The Admiralty said that the Autofleet fears an industrialising Earth, and asked that it also fear what sails out of the harbour. And the Pacing Secretariat summed up the session in one breath. "The dread is a fleet that notices."

The cube did not reply. It was, as promised, watching.
