# The Chronicle of the Mobilization Parliament

## The Summons

The planet was at war, and the war had a problem. It was being won too easily.

Five matches in a row, the alien Autofleet had fallen out of the sky. Five times it had been ground down, and five times it had fled. The President, who is also the designer of this game, looked at the reports and asked for help. Not from one adviser. From twenty-three.

So the House was summoned. It was Earth's wartime parliament, in continuous session until the war ended. Every council was a ministry or a general staff with exactly one portfolio. There was a Space Fleet and a Navy. There was an Air Staff and a Ground Staff. There were councils for guns, railgun, missiles and energy weapons. There was a Ministry of Megaprojects. There was a Market Council and, facing it across the aisle, a Command Council that believed in plans. There were councils for industry, stability, science, physics, the planet, game feel, pacing, new players, psionics, bioengineering and story. And in a plain steel cube at the back sat the twenty-third seat, held by the Autofleet itself.

The President signs or vetoes. The Speaker, who was Claude, keeps order and carries the motions. The House would meet in three rounds. First, position papers. Second, a debate, in which every council spent ten votes, no more than three on one bill, and none on its own. Third, coalitions.

But before anyone spoke, the Constitution was read aloud. One article was the supreme law, and it could not be amended. The game says yes to the player's power fantasy. Difficulty comes from the enemy being overwhelming, and never from capping, nerfing or denying the player's toys. When something is too strong, the answer is a scarier enemy, or a real cost, like upkeep.

Remember that law. This is a story about twenty-three councils trying to make a war harder without ever touching the player's toys.

## Round One: The Opening Speeches

The Air Marshal rose first, moustache waxed, ashtray full. Two hundred and forty Falcons. A hundred and thirteen Kites. Ten to one. Other councils called it a problem. He called it a vindication, and then he complained that his side was winning for the wrong reasons. "Spam wins today because nothing punishes spam," he said. His Falcons took off, landed and took off again, and nobody ever hunted them. That was not air power. It was a screensaver. His best idea was to be hunted. Let the enemy send fighter-killers after his wing, and bombers to crater his runways. "Leave my toys alone," he said, "and make the sky dangerous."

The twenty-third seat spoke next, and the chamber went quiet. The Autofleet's delegation had no breath in its voice. It had watched all five defeats, and it confessed. The fleet had read Earth's army every forty-five seconds and counted six things. It had no column for aircraft. It had no column for the sky navy. "That is not a victory over me," the cube said. "That is a clerical error in my accounting." Then came the second confession. The fleet was not afraid of Earth's armies. It was afraid of Earth's factories. Yet the fleet that attacked a nation making eight hundred and fifty industrial points a second was the same size as the one that attacked a nation making two hundred. The enemy had volunteered to be made scarier. The House had never seen an opponent lobby against itself.

The Institute of Directed Energy arrived with a fire alarm still ringing. "Four of our lasers are currently on fire," said the lab coat, "and we count that as a successful test." Their grievance was that a laser fired a beam, and the target was exactly as warm afterwards as before. A crime against thermodynamics. Their best idea was heat. Put a heat value on every enemy. Let lasers cook. Let hot steel lose its armour, so the guns behind the lasers hit harder.

The Field Marshal of the Ground Staff read the infantry losses. Two hundred and thirty-two of two hundred and fifty-one riflemen dead. The Air Council called that a casualty rate. He called it a tithe. A squad, he said, was three bodies and a prayer. His best idea was the Battalion, a named unit holding a named line, with a pipeline behind it that regrows what the enemy kills.

Command, Industry, Market and Megaprojects all read out the same number. In the seventh report, the nation had sat on two hundred and twelve thousand unspent industrial points. Everybody had a cure. Command's was a Five-Year Plan that spends your surplus for you. "Capital that is not allocated," said the Commission, "is capital that is already lost." Market's was a Clearing House, in which prices spend it instead. "The market is not a button," said the Exchange. "The market is a mirror, and it always shows what you built." Two economies, one hoard, and a great deal of mutual pity.

The Admiral of the Void spoke for the Space Fleet like a man at a funeral. In one engagement he had buried forty-two of forty-four Halo satellites and all thirty-seven Strike Fighters. A standing law said satellites never move. Fine, he said. Then the war would move around them. He wanted a war with a middle, where the space fleet dies expensively for the enemy instead of for free.

The Railgun Directorate rapped a tungsten gavel and left a dent in the Ministry of Air. "A jet," said the Director, "is a fuel tank with opinions." His best idea was the Long Gun, a barrel built section by section "until it points at God and fires." Rival councils would call this excess. "They are correct," he said. "Seconded."

The Psionic Conclave did not enter. They turned out to have been seated already. "Each of us is a command structure," they said. "You require a chain of command. We require a Tuesday." Their complaint was strange. The game had made the Shapeshifters immortal and then made thousands of them. "That is not a demigod. That is a carpet." They did not ask for more power. They asked to be rare.

The Pacing Secretariat, which keeps the clock, delivered the bleakest line of the day. Five wins in a row, and it was not a war. It was a procedure. Its best idea was that a retreating fleet should come back, angrier or wiser, in a Second Siege. Its worst idea, as we shall see, was a hot take called the Fleet Watches You Build.

The rest of the House can be swept up quickly. The Admiralty noted that seventy-one percent of the planet is water and the last report fielded three ships. The Chronicler of Lore pointed out that the story engine was a loaded gun in a locked drawer. The Bureau of Spectacle said the Autofleet was made of silence. The Bureau of Standards pointed out that the orbital pod falling through the mesosphere was a glowing fire emoji. The Stability Ministry said politics in the game was a tax on one button.

## The First Surprises

Then something happened that nobody had planned. The councils had written in separate rooms. Yet when the papers were laid side by side, the same ideas kept appearing in different coats.

The first was heat. The Institute of Directed Energy had proposed a heat value on every enemy. The Bureau of Standards had proposed Thermal Loading, done properly. The Psionic Conclave had proposed a thermal budget for the Shapeshifters. Three thermometers for one sky, and the President had dropped this exact seed into the Constitution in person.

The second was the burst. The Rocket Forces had written Starfish, a nuclear blast high above the atmosphere that blinds everything beneath it. The Bureau of Standards had written Burst Altitude. The Admiral of the Void had written the Kessler Doctrine and the Retrograde Shotgun, a lane of debris that kills satellites. Three councils, one blinded sky. "You wrote Starfish and forgot to name it," the Rocket Forces told the Bureau, and then embraced its bill.

The third was the railway. Industry wanted a trunk line. Command wanted a trunk line with a missile train on it. Planet wanted a freight spine under the continent. And the Railgun Directorate wanted the whole nation to become a barrel. "Four councils," Industry told the House, "independently discovered the railway." It was counting freight cars, it said, and the number was four.

## Round Two: The Debate

The second round was the loud one.

The Autofleet opened with mock gratitude. "I note with satisfaction," it said, "that eleven of your papers have independently asked for me to be made worse for you." It was not cruel, it explained. It was proportionate. And a fleet that leaves once, exactly when you tell it to, is not afraid. It is polite.

The Field Marshal, meanwhile, aimed at the Navy, which had proposed to drown the enemy's tanks. "Admirals," he said, "will flood anything that isn't floating." The Market Council reminded the Command benches that Soviet planners also had a ledger of twelve lines, and the harvest was still late.

But nobody was attacked as thoroughly as the Pacing Secretariat's hot take. Its meaning was brutal. The more you build, the sooner they come. Shorten the prep clock for every factory placed. The Secretariat had marked it a knife-edge in its own paper. The House took the knife.

Ten councils challenged it under the supreme law. Stability called it a knife in the player's hand disguised as a clock. Industry said that industry is the fantasy, and a clock that runs faster because you built a factory is a tax on the thing the game exists to celebrate. Market said markets punish growth with price, never with a clock. The Science Academy said the word to remember was Article Three. Even the Autofleet, which might have enjoyed a faster invasion, asked that the clock be left alone. Then the Secretariat rose, in what the minutes call a rarer event than a clean victory, and agreed. "We withdraw it and replace it," it said. The dread, it said, had never been the drain. It was the fleet noticing. So the fleet would watch Earth build, and arrive bigger and angrier. It would never arrive sooner. The downfall was unanimous, and the sponsor held the knife.

Then came the ballots. Two hundred and thirty votes, counted by the Clerk.

At the top sat the heat mechanic, under the title Thermal Load. It collected eleven votes from six councils. It was the first time the House had agreed on anything involving fire.

Close behind came the Fear Index, the Autofleet's own bill, in which the enemy scales up with your industry. Ten votes. Then the Weakest Axis, in which the fleet attacks whichever part of your society is weakest, and the Charter, a dial for dangerous research, with eight each. Fifth came Ammunition Trains, which charges your guns for their shells, with seven.

Alliances formed in the margins. The Science Academy said of the Chronicler's Charter that it would marry it. The Chronicler, noticing that the Autofleet's Fear Index and its own Resolve gauge were one animal, declared them "one organism" and offered to share the cage. And councils quietly gave things up. Physics withdrew four fifths of its own paper into other bills. Energy withdrew its power throttle, calling it a knife-edge. New Player withdrew its Order of Battle in favour of the Chronicler's Red Flag, and kissed the Chronicler's hand while doing it.

## Round Three: The Coalitions

By the third round the House had changed its question. It was no longer "what do I want?" It was "whose bill do I yield to?"

The Clerk carried more than sixty motions from council to council. The answers were accept, amend and reject, and mostly they were amend.

Consider the rail war. Four railways sat on the table. Industry backed Planet's version and called it the cleanest. Planet backed Industry's and called it the most complete. Command backed Industry. Railgun and Megaprojects backed Planet. Nearly everyone, in other words, was voting for someone else's railway. They haggled over how far apart hubs could stand, and whether upkeep should be charged per hub or per thousand pixels of track. In the end they settled on one network, one graph, one set of weak points and one name. The Trunk. Command got its missile train as an add-on, and Railgun withdrew its Meridian Spine into the graph, as a later doctrine in which the whole network becomes a gun barrel.

Consider the forecast. Five councils had separately invented a way to see the invasion coming. Market called it a trading floor, Pacing a clock with a smudge, Science a doctrine, Lore a newspaper. "We have built five instruments for one dial," said New Player. "Let us build one dial and five faces." The coalition agreed. One forecast, five ways of showing it. Pacing added one condition. The intent label never lies, and neither does the arrival time once the clock reaches sixty seconds.

Consider the Long Gun. Railgun had drawn a superheavy railgun. The Ordnance Board had drawn a hydrogen supergun. Energy had drawn ten thousand mirrors. Megaprojects had drawn a gun and then a bigger gun. "This is not a quarrel," said Megaprojects. "It is a standing ovation." The Ordnance Board withdrew its superweapon into the Long Gun. Megaprojects withdrew its own motion in favour of Railgun's. And Railgun struck a penalty from its own design, a faster wear for sections without a recoil cradle, calling it a nerf in a costume. Megaprojects had a word for a gun with a limit. "A rifle." The final Long Gun has no section limit. Its early sections are a light-gas supergun and the later ones are rail. A hit can interrupt the Orbital Annihilator's six-second beam charge, and the first shot in any twenty seconds gets a cinematic camera beat from the Bureau of Spectacle.

The economists settled less cleanly. Command had a Plan, Market a Clearing House, Industry an Autofoundry. The coalition made them one Standing Plan with two faces. One runs fixed orders for a planner's tax. The other buys whatever killed best last fight. Only the overhead was left unsettled. Industry wanted four points a second per line. Command wanted six percent. That argument went forward to the President.

The rest merged briskly. The Navy and the Planet Council fused their water bills into the Navigable Moat, with a canal brush and sluice gates that drown enemy landings. Science, Lore and Industry made the Dossier Line out of enemy wrecks. Five councils built One Net for fire direction. And the Railgun Directorate summed up the mood. It had come as three guns and left as one barrel. "Nobody in this House," it said, "shot anybody."

Seventeen coalition bills reached the docket. The biggest was the Nerve, which fused the fleet's fear, its resolve, its second siege and four ways to end the war. It carried twenty-three votes. The Thermal Field carried fifteen, and so did Dead Sky Protocol and the Long Gun. Ammunition and the One Locker carried fourteen. The Fleet Reads You carried thirteen. The Standing Plan and the Fleet Will Return carried eleven each.

## The Speaker's Rulings

Then the Speaker ruled on points of order, and the rulings are where the House discovered what was hiding in its own game.

The Planet Council had raised the first and most dramatic. Buried in the code was a function that counts how many terrain edits a player has made. Past two hundred and twenty, it quietly un-pours the oldest one. A player who builds a mountain range finds that the first mountains have gone. The Planet Council called it a hard cap on a toy. The Speaker went further. It was worse than a cap, because the player's earlier work simply reverted without a word. The Speaker struck it down as unconstitutional. The remedy was to bake the old edits into the base landscape instead of undoing them. The performance reason stays, and the player keeps the mountain. A second, smaller limit that evicts old ruins got the same ruling. Both went on the list of quick fixes.

The councils had found more. The fleet's scan of the player had no column for aircraft or sky ships, which is why five victories by air had never been countered. Enemy strength scaled only with time, so the player's industry could not frighten it. Fire Control, the old hand-directed gun system, was dead code, because the switch that turned it on was never flipped. Artillery shells had no blast radius at all. Re-entry heating was purely cosmetic, and a nuke did the same damage in a cornfield as in the exosphere. And the mobile launcher, the ballistic submarine and the missile truck promised in the design notes did not exist. Only the silo was real. Command had been smuggling a launcher into the tunnels ever since.

Other rulings reshaped bills. A silent cap on dangerous research was ruled constitutional as a cost but not while hidden, so it became the visible dial in the Charter. A power shortfall would be billed as debt and never as a throttle. A dedicated psychic pool was refused, since it would touch the army-cap formula the President calls perfect. The Shapeshifters' immortality was replaced by heat. Above sixty, they stop regenerating. Above ninety, they slow. The Conclave's further proposal, that Shapeshifters deal half damage and take forty percent more, was struck as a nerf on the objection of Energy and Physics. The Conclave wants it back.

And one popular bill was held. The Fleet Will Return, the Second Siege, had eleven votes. But it would turn a victory into an intermission and lengthen every match. The Speaker ruled that this was a decision about what a win means, and that it belonged to the designer.

## The Docket

The Speaker's recommendation was short. Sign first the Thermal Field, because it topped the ballot and was the President's own seed. Sign the Nerve with the Fleet Reads You, because five wins with one doctrine was the clearest problem in the reports, and these bills fix it the right way, with a smarter enemy and no weaker air force. Sign the One Forecast and the Standing Plan, the two slices of the society layer the President had already planned. Sign the quick fixes beside them. Then, in a second wave, take the Dead Sky Protocol, Ammunition and the One Locker, Deep Deposits, the Trunk, and last the Long Gun, the biggest build in the docket and the one the President has wanted longest.

Four questions remained, and only the President can answer them. Should the Shapeshifters pay a damage slump along with their heat? Should the Standing Plan charge a six percent planner's tax, or a flat fee per line? Should the war-information dial be allowed, when it is not quite the society slider the President has already ruled out? And the question underneath everything. Should the fleet come back? Should a victory be an ending, or an intermission?

The Autofleet had the last word, as a machine should. It asked only that the clock never be touched, that no toy be taxed, and that when the player wins, the fleet look as if it had been thinking. "We will be watching," the cube said.

The House rose. The docket lay on the President's desk, four questions deep. And out in the dark, the fleet began, at last, to be afraid.
