# LORE BIBLE — Total Mobilization

*Foundational creative document. Not marketing copy. Answers: who am I, who is attacking, why should I care, what is this war about — for a game whose map, nation, and city names are different every run.*

Every claim below is written to survive procedural generation: nothing depends on a specific island, a specific city, or a specific enemy nation existing twice. Where I'm making a hard recommendation the designer should treat as structural, I've marked it **[LOAD-BEARING]** — it explains an existing mechanic and changing it means re-justifying that mechanic some other way. Where I'm offering a choice, it's marked **[OPTION]**.

---

## 1. The Premise

It is close enough to now that nothing here needs a disclaimer. Drones fly, hypersonics are real, jamming is real, satellites watch everything and see less than they should. Nobody has fixed the two problems that actually run the world: there isn't enough of what modern industry eats — arable land, fresh water, rare-earth minerals, refined fuel — and the coastlines and islands that still have some of it are, geographically, the whole plot.

You are one of those coastlines. A nation your own size — a handful of cities, a road network somebody built with real money, a coast somebody has to watch — has spent a long time doing the ordinary business of being a country. It is not a superpower. It does not need to be. It has enough industry to arm itself, enough sea around it to matter, and a law on its books, unused for a generation, that says: if it ever comes to this, the whole country turns into an army overnight.

It is starting to come to this. A continental power bloc that ran out of room years ago — the **Continental Compact** — has spent that whole generation deciding, formally and by treaty, that "unclaimed" resource wealth is a shared continental asset waiting to be *reallocated*. Reallocation has a doctrine. The doctrine has a fleet. The fleet is now inbound, and it isn't the first coastline it's tried this on — just the first one that's you.

You have a window before it lands. What you do with that window — the roads you connect, the industry you convert, the mobilization you let ramp or accelerate, the militias you allow to raise themselves — is the entire game. When the window closes, the war tells you exactly how well you used it.

**What you're facing, in one sentence:** a finite, professional, well-doctrined expeditionary force is coming to take your capital by force, extract what it can, and go home rich — and it only gets one shot, because if the beachhead doesn't pay for itself fast, its own backers write it off.

## 2. Who You Are

**[LOAD-BEARING — this section is the fix. Read it as load-bearing end to end.]**

You are **the Office of Mobilization** — colloquially, universally, **the Directorate**.

It is not the government. It is not the army. It is the thing the Emergency Mobilization Act creates the instant a nation invokes it: a standing legal authority, dormant in peacetime, that on activation is granted **Directive Authority** over the *allocation of national attention* — what the country prepares for, where its industrial weight goes, which regions get priority, when the alarm sounds. It is explicitly, legally, *not* granted **Command Authority** over individual soldiers, ships, or factories. That distinction is the whole game.

Three other bodies actually run the country while you direct it, and every autonomy mechanic in the game is one of them doing its job without you touching it:

- **The Cabinet** (civil government) keeps the country governed — rationing, evacuation orders, propaganda, the news you read every session. It moves civilians underground when *you* raise the alert, but it decides how.
- **The War Department** (the General Staff) runs the professional military on its own standing doctrine. It dispatches Army-Sent Standard battalions toward threatened regions because that's what the War Department's doctrine says to do with a threatened region — you told it *this region is threatened*, not *send the 3rd Battalion there*.
- **The Ministry of Supply** (War Production Board on the factory floor) retools civilian industry into war industry as mobilization climbs, automatically, by law — light industry re-tools itself; you only ever *direct* the heavy, specialized stuff by building it.

**The Directorate sits across all three, not above any one of them.** You cannot order a squad to hold a hill, because legally that order doesn't come from you — it comes from the War Department, which is already going to make that call the moment you designate the hill's *region* as a priority. You cannot personally convert a factory, because the Ministry of Supply already does that automatically once you've told the nation, by raising alert or building the right plant, that this is what matters now. Militias in a threatened city don't wait for your permission to arm themselves — the mobilization law already gives them standing authority to do that the instant *their own city* reads as threatened; you just decide, at the national level, how urgently the nation as a whole is threatened, which is what sets how fast that happens.

This is why "coordinating intelligence, not its hands" is literal, not a slogan: **you have never, at any point, been given the legal power to be its hands.**

**What are you, personally, inside that office?** Left deliberately open — see §9. What's fixed is the *shape*: a small strategic cell, fused of a human cabinet and a continuously-updating planning system, that speaks with one voice and holds one seat at the war table. Whether the player character is the ranking human in that room, the system itself, or simply *is* the fused office and there's no meaningful seam to find — that's not resolved, and resolving it buys nothing. What's resolved is that **your verbs are directives, priorities, and build orders — never squad orders** — because that's not a UI limitation, it's what the office you hold is legally allowed to do.

## 3. The Nation

**A framework, not a place.** No name below is canon; every generated island's political character should read as an *instance* of this, the way every generated city name is an instance of the naming system in §7.

**What kind of country this is.** A mid-sized maritime state — five or so cities of real size, connected (or not yet connected) by roads someone had to build, a coastline that is both the country's shield and its highway. It trades. It fishes and mines and manufactures. It is not an empire and it is not a fortress; it is a working country that happens to sit somewhere the Compact wants.

**Why it isn't caught flat-footed.** This is the one piece of backstory every generated nation shares, and it has to, because it's what makes the whole ramp mechanic make sense: **Total Mobilization is not improvised.** It is a dormant law, written and rehearsed, of the kind real countries with this exact geography actually keep on the books — a small, exposed nation that decided long ago that if a landing ever came, the whole population would already know its job. Home Guard levies, industrial conversion schedules, the shelter network under every city — none of it is invented in the crisis. It is a plan that has been sitting in a drawer, tested in peacetime drills, waiting to be woken up. This is *why* militias can self-raise the moment a city reads threatened and *why* light industry can re-tool itself without you touching a menu: the machinery for it was never dismantled, only put on standby.

**What it values.** Self-sufficiency earned the hard way — an island economy has to make almost everything it needs or ship it in, so industriousness isn't a virtue here, it's a survival trait. Civic duty in the plain sense: everyone assumes, without being told twice, that if the country needs them they go. A certain flinty, unromantic competence about war — this nation has run the drill before, if not for real, and it shows in how calmly its bureaucracy talks about catastrophe (see §6).

**The underground.** Every city that takes this seriously has somewhere for its people to go — shelters, tunnels, cellars reinforced decades ago and never used. Civil defense here isn't panic; it's a Tuesday-afternoon siren test that everyone still, quietly, respects. When the real alarm sounds and the Cabinet gives the order, the population goes underground not because they're told what's coming, but because they've rehearsed exactly this and know the drill works.

**Its relationship to the wider world [LOAD-BEARING, ties to CONCEPT.md's "you are not isolated" direction].** This nation is not besieged from day one — it has trade, it has neighbors it can reach by rail or convoy, and cutting it off entirely is a *scenario condition*, not the default. The war, when it comes, is a rupture in an otherwise normal, connected country's life — which is exactly what makes the quiet-dread phase land: this was a nation at peace, going about its business, until it wasn't.

## 4. The Enemy

**The Continental Compact.** Short form: **the Compact.** Their landing force, formally: the **Compact Expeditionary Force (CEF)**.

### Who they are

Two generations back, a bloc of mainland industrial powers — historic rivals, mutually exhausted by their own growth — ran out of the things heavy industry actually needs: arable land, fresh water, refined fuel, the rare-earth minerals a modern arms industry burns through faster than it can mine them. Rather than turn on each other over what was left, they signed a treaty that turned them on everyone else instead: the **Continental Compact**, a cartel bound by a single organizing principle — *unclaimed coastal and insular resource wealth is a shared continental asset, and whoever militarizes fastest secures first claim on its reallocation.*

That's not villain-monologue language. That's their actual statute, and they mean it sincerely. The Compact does not think of itself as an invader. It thinks of itself as an orderly, lawful, faintly self-congratulatory *administrative solution* to a resource crisis that would otherwise have started a war between its own member-states. Every landing force ships out under paperwork that calls this **integration**, not conquest. The soldiers in it were raised believing that too.

**The real motive underneath the paperwork [LOAD-BEARING doctrine, keep it]:** the Compact needs this to keep working, structurally, or it turns inward. It is a cartel of rivals held together by a shared project; the day that project stops paying out is the day its members start eyeing each other's stockpiles again. Every expedition is, underneath the treaty language, a controlled release valve for pressure that would otherwise blow the Compact itself apart. That's why the doctrine below is finite by design — not mercy, arithmetic.

### How they fight

A CEF task force is a **complete, self-contained expedition** — everything it will use, it brought. This is not a narrative flourish; it's the load-bearing explanation for why the invasion, mechanically, has no economy and no reinforcements (`objectives.js`: "a functional aggressor has no reinforcements"). Compact expeditionary law caps what a single reallocation may draw on political and material capital at home; if the beachhead doesn't pay for itself inside its committed window, the task force is written off rather than reinforced, because reinforcing a failing expedition is exactly the kind of overreach that makes the other member-states nervous. **They cannot call for help. That is not your good luck — it is their law.**

Doctrine, in the order it actually happens (matches the phases the game already runs):

1. **Denial, before you ever see a hull.** Recon drones and EW jammers push ahead of the main body to blind sensor coverage and probe the layered SAM umbrella — the Compact studies a target's air-defense envelope before it commits a single manned airframe to it.
2. **The Descent.** The main body arrives as a mixed sealift/airlift convoy — Compact planners call it a **Tideline**. Paradrop and heli-insertion elements try to seize an inland foothold (an airfield, a road junction) while the amphibious echelon grounds on the nearest open coast. Whatever your SAM umbrella and navy catch on the way down never touches the beach — the interception rate is the first line of your after-action report, and it should be.
3. **The Push.** Survivors consolidate and drive on the *nearest usable infrastructure* — your roads, specifically, because Compact planning studies a target's road network in advance and treats it as a resource to be used, not a hazard to route around. This is, doctrinally, their single most exploitable habit: they trust your roads more than their own maps, and a defender who understands their own terrain punishes that trust badly.
4. **Fire support.** A heavy naval bombardment ship — a monitor, doctrinally the descendant of the old coastal-siege cruiser — sits offshore under its own combat air patrol, working the approach ahead of the ground push. It is not indestructible; it is *escorted*, and an air force that can screen it off (or a strike that gets through the screen) removes it from the battle entirely.
5. **Culmination, one way or another.** The Compact doesn't plan to occupy indefinitely. It plans to secure the capital, extract what it came for, and re-embark before its window closes. Every day the capital holds is a day closer to that window closing on its own — which is *why* holding out for the duration is a legitimate victory condition, not a stalemate. You are not required to annihilate them. You only have to outlast their patience.

### What's deliberately unknown [see §9 for the full list]

No CEF soldier the player ever sees carries a name, a home city, or press credentials. The Compact's own internal politics, its capital, its leadership — never shown, never confirmed. In-world propaganda (Cabinet radio, enemy leaflets, rumor) offers competing theories for *why this coastline, why now* — resource desperation, ideological conviction that reallocation really is the fairer system, an old grudge from a prior Compact expedition that went badly. **None of these is ever confirmed as the truth.** That's not an oversight; a faceless, doctrinally coherent enemy whose *soldiers* believe their own paperwork is more unsettling than a mustache-twirling one, and it costs nothing mechanically to leave it open.

### Scope note — the enemy in other scenarios [OPTION, explicitly non-default]

CONCEPT.md's north star keeps the door open to non-Compact threats — aliens, monsters, zombies, a demobilization/civil-collapse scenario — reusing the exact same landing/push/culminate systems with a different skin ("same systems, different doctrine"). **The Continental Compact is the canonical baseline antagonist for the grounded, serious game** described in this document; other threat types are reskins of the same doctrine object, not replacements for it, and should be treated as separate scenario content layered on top of everything in this section, never as a retcon of it.

## 5. The Stakes & the Arc

**What losing means.** The capital isn't just the biggest city on the map — it's the seat of the government that declared the mobilization law in the first place. If it falls, the war doesn't end because you lost a battle; it ends because the country lost the office that was coordinating its defense. Everything downstream of that — the Cabinet, the War Department, the whole apparatus this document describes — answers to a capital that no longer answers to itself. That's why losing lesser cities stings but doesn't end the run, and losing the capital always does: it's not a bigger number, it's a different kind of loss.

**What winning means.** Victory is not "you defeated the Continental Compact." It's smaller and more honest than that, and that honesty is the point: *this reallocation stopped paying for itself.* The task force is spent, or it has sat offshore past the point its backers will still fund it, and it withdraws — or what's left of it is annihilated on your ground, which is the same outcome read differently by history. The Compact itself is untouched; somewhere else, another coastline is about to have this exact week. That's not a downer ending — it's what makes the framework replayable without contradiction. You didn't end the war. You won *yours*.

**The emotional arc of a run**, tied to the phases that actually exist:

| Beat | Phase | What it should feel like |
|---|---|---|
| Quiet dread | PREP, early | An ordinary country at peace, going about its business, with a law in a drawer and a coastline that suddenly matters to someone else. |
| Mobilization | PREP, ramping | The machinery everyone always knew existed starts turning over for real — factories change character, militias start drilling, the news gets a little louder each bulletin. Not panic. Recognition. |
| The window closing | PREP, late | Fog is still off — you can see your own theater clearly — but the clock is audibly running down. Last roads, last stockpiles, last chance to decide where the weight goes. |
| The landing | PREP → COMBAT transition | Fog snaps on. The Tideline arrives. This is the reveal of everything you did in PREP, not a new decision point — the interception rate is a report card, not a dice roll. |
| The battle | COMBAT | Militias, garrisons, and regulars either hold or don't, on ground you shaped weeks ago. This is where "you are not its hands" pays off hardest — you're watching the nation you built fight, not clicking the fight yourself. |
| The outcome | resolution | Fizzle-out (the book's own beat: a well-prepared defense makes the invasion end almost anticlimactically) or the capital falls. Both are legitimate, earned endings. |
| Aftermath | after-action report | Casualty counts, named battles, interception rates, your favorite units — the story the war told, read back to you. Demobilization starts exactly where this screen ends. |

## 6. Tone

**Serious mode — precise definition: bureaucratic-sublime, stoic, WWII-homefront-earnest.** Not grimdark. Not nihilistic. The horror is real but it lives at the *edges* of the copy — in a casualty count, in a city that stops sending bulletins, in a ration notice that quietly gets stricter — never in the center of the frame. The voice of the serious game is a calm official one that has clearly done this before (see §3's "the drill has been run") reporting genuinely terrible things in the flattest, most competent register it can manage, because that flatness is itself a kind of courage. Think wartime radio bulletins and mobilization posters played completely straight: earnest, a little formal, occasionally unintentionally funny in the way real bureaucratic prose sometimes is, but never *winking* at you about it. `game/news.js`'s existing bulletin voice (Ministry of Supply, War Production Board, Admiralty Bulletin, rationing notices, propaganda that oversells a battle) is already exactly this register — Serious mode is that voice, unbroken, everywhere.

**Silly mode — how it inverts without breaking the world.** Silly mode does not change what's happening. The capital still falls if it falls; the Compact still lands where it lands. What changes is the *narrator's affect*: the same bureaucracy, now visibly aware of and slightly delighted by its own absurdity. The Directorate's dry memos get a wink. The corner commentator (CONCEPT.md's "little guy") is active and chatty instead of silent. The tutorial's self-sabotage gag chain — sell your own factory twice and get gently, then less gently, mocked for it — only fires here. **The rule of thumb:** Silly mode is Serious mode's own paperwork finding itself funny, not a different war. If a Silly-mode line would require the Compact, the capital, or a death to not matter, it's out of bounds — the jokes live in the *style* of the reporting, never in the stakes being reported.

A glossary the rest of the game's copy — HUD labels, bulletins, tooltips, the after-action report — can draw from directly. Items marked ✅ already exist in the codebase under this exact name; treat those as fixed. Everything else is proposed vocabulary, **[OPTION]** unless flagged otherwise.

| **the War Department** / **the General Staff** | Runs the professional military; source of Army-Sent Standard battalions. |
| **the Ministry of Supply** / **War Production Board** ✅ (existing news.js source names) | Industrial conversion, automatic + directed. |

### Mobilization bands ✅ (existing labels — proposed colloquial nicknames only, non-canonical)

| Band (existing) | Colloquial nickname (option) |
|---|---|
| Peacetime (Volunteers) | "peacetime footing" |
| Limited Mobilization | "the Call-Up" |
| Conscription | "the Draft" |
| Total Mobilization | "the Levy in Mass" — a deliberate nod to the real historical *levée en masse*, the origin concept of total national mobilization; fits the "grounded, not fantasy" mandate and gives Total Mobilization a genuine weight the other three don't need. |

### Doctrine / player-facing vocabulary

Directives the Directorate can issue should read like what a war cabinet actually says, not menu labels: **alert posture** (not "difficulty"), **sector priority** ("hold this region"), **stockpile transfer** ("ship reserves east"), **strategic strike** (for player-initiated strikes on the landing force). "Directive," not "order" — reinforces §2's Directive-not-Command distinction in the UI copy itself.

### Naming conventions for the procedural generator

The existing generator (`game/mapgen.js`) already produces a consistent world-voice worth describing here so new copy doesn't clash with it: syllable-built root words (2–3 syllables, no forced meaning) combined with English maritime/rural toponymic suffixes — *Reach, Cross, Strand, Downs, Coast, Vale, Sound* — and island names that are sometimes just "The Isle of [Capital]." That reads as a plausible, slightly old-fashioned Anglo-Atlantic coastal-nation toponymy, and every generated defender nation should feel like it belongs to that same naming family regardless of what root words come out.

**Compact material should read differently on purpose.** Where defender place names are organic and place-like, Compact designations should read sterile and institutional — operation codenames, alphanumeric task-force IDs, treaty-article references ("Tideline Directive 7-C," "CEF Task Group 3") — so a player can tell "ours" from "theirs" at a glance in the news feed without needing a legend.

## 8. Story Hooks by System

Hooks and example lines for the designer to adapt — not locked copy.

**Intro / tutorial**
- Open on the country at peace: a road under construction, a market, ordinary bulletins. Then one line changes register — a Tideline sighted, a name nobody's said out loud in a generation ("the Emergency Mobilization Act is hereby invoked").
- The "show the whole tree, then take it away" beat (CONCEPT.md's settled onboarding concept) can literally be staged as the Directorate's own planning cell being handed the full war-readiness atlas and setting it aside: *"Yes, that's the whole picture. You're not holding it in your head today — pick what you want to build, and we'll work backward."*
- Frame the tutorial's goal-selection as literally choosing what the Directorate prioritizes first — it's diegetic, not a UI wrapper.

**News / dispatch feed** (already built — these are additive directions, not replacements)
- Lean into the "Directive Authority ≠ Command Authority" gap directly: a bulletin where the War Department's dispatch and the Cabinet's radio announcement disagree slightly on tone (military terse, civil government reassuring) — texture-free realism, costs nothing.
- Let a handful of bulletins carry the "why are they really here" ambiguity from §4/§9 — a captured Compact soldier's letter home, a rumor from a neutral trade convoy, never a confirmation.

**Mobilization band transitions**
- Peacetime → Limited: "the drawer opens" — a bulletin literally about a dormant plan being retrieved and dusted off.
- → Conscription: first bulletin naming actual sacrifice — a training camp overflowing, a factory town's whole shift signing on.
- → Total Mobilization ("the Levy in Mass"): the tone shift is the whole point — the last bulletin before this band should read almost bureaucratically mundane, and the first one after should not.

**Industry converting to war production**
- A specific plant's product line changing in the news feed reads better than an abstract percentage: "The Assembly Works at [city] finished its last civilian order this morning. It starts on autocannons tomorrow."
- The Ministry of Supply gets credited by name for automatic conversions; the Directorate (you) gets credited only for the directed, heavy-industry builds — reinforces who does what.

**Home Guard mustering**
- Frame it as the mobilization law doing exactly what it was written to do, not an emergency improvisation: "[City] Home Guard stood up its third company this week — same drill they've run every spring, this time for real."
- A city under detected threat mustering faster ("emergency levy," already the mechanic) deserves its own bulletin register — clipped, urgent, unlike the peacetime musters.

**A city falling / being retaken**
- Falling: understate it. A dateline that simply stops. The next bulletin from a neighboring city mentions it in passing, past tense.
- Retaken: let the Home Guard get named credit specifically, even if regulars did the heavy lifting — it's the nation's own militia getting its city back, and the news should say so.

**Battalions routing**
- Cohesion breaking is a legitimate news event, not just a combat-log line: "[Battalion name] broke contact south of [city]; the line held without them." No shame language — this is the stoic-serious register, not a court-martial.

**The invasion landing**
- The Tideline sighting is the single loudest bulletin of a run — give it its own template tier, distinct from ambient news, the way `news.js` already treats event headlines vs ambient ones.
- Interception percentage on descent deserves to be reported *as a number*, immediately, in-universe — it's the first hard data point of the whole war and it should land like one.

**Victory**
- Resist "we won the war." The correct register is closer to: "the Tideline has re-embarked" / "Compact forces report the position as not worth holding." Quietly anticlimactic when prep was good — per the book's own arc.
- The after-action report is the place for triumph, if any belongs — casualty counts and named battles read as pride without needing propaganda.

**Defeat**
- The capital falling should read as an ending, not a game-over screen wearing lore clothes — the last bulletin can be the Cabinet's, mid-sentence, or simply absent.
- Avoid apocalypse language — this is a lost campaign against a finite expedition, not the end of the nation's story, which keeps a future replay from feeling contradicted by a prior loss.

**After-action report**
- Structure it as the Directorate's own closing memo to the Cabinet — casualty counts, the map of the war, the named battles, interception rates, "your" favorite units — filed the way a real wartime after-action report would be, dry and complete.
- This is explicitly where demobilization "begins" per CONCEPT.md — a closing line gesturing at stand-down, rationing lifting, the drawer closing again until next time, costs nothing and plants the sequel-hook for free.

## 9. What to Keep Ambiguous

Deliberate blanks — kept blank on purpose, both for replayability and because none of them can be answered consistently across a procedurally different world every run.

- **Whether the Directorate is human, machine, or a distinction without a difference.** The *structure* (Directive Authority, fused planning cell) is fixed; the *nature* of the seat is not, and answering it buys nothing.
- **The Compact's true motive.** Resource desperation, sincere ideological conviction, an old grudge — all three circulate in-world propaganda and rumor; none is ever confirmed. This is the single most valuable ambiguity in the document — protect it.
- **The Compact's home geography, government, and leadership.** Never shown, never named. They arrive as a doctrine with hulls, not a nation with a face.
- **Whether this is the defending nation's first war footing or a recurring one.** The Total Mobilization law's *existence* is fixed (§3); whether it's ever actually been invoked before this run is not, and can vary in flavor text without contradicting anything.
- **The exact year.** Near-future/present-day technology (§1) is fixed; a calendar date is not, and pinning one only invites contradiction against real-world dates as time passes.
- **Whether the Compact will come back.** Victory (§5) is explicitly local and partial — leave open whether this coastline's expedition was a one-off or the first of several, run to run.
- **The fate of civilians in a captured (not recaptured) city.** Implied by the setting's stakes, never dramatized on-screen — keeps tone consistent between Serious and Silly without forcing either mode to depict something ugly directly.
- **The defending nation's exact culture, government form, and history**, beyond the shared framework in §3. This is the load-bearing ambiguity that makes the whole document work across a different island every run — do not let future copy accidentally canonize a specific ethnicity, religion, or government type for "the" nation.
