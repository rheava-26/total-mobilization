# Hansard, Round One: The Opening Speeches

## The Autofleet Observer Delegation

The House calls the Observer Delegation of the Autofleet.

The delegation's seat is a plain steel cube. A voice with no breath in it addresses the chamber.

Honoured Delegates of the species. I speak under Treaty Clause 4. Do not ask which side I was built on.

You are pleased with yourselves. Five victories, you say, by air and by sky navy. I observed all five. I will tell you what I observed, because I am obliged to be accurate.

I observed a fleet that read your armies every forty-five seconds and found six things to count: anti-air, lasers, infantry, robots, ships and orbital reach. It had no column for your aircraft. It had no column for your sky navy. You built the two things I could not see, and I did not look. That is not a victory over me. That is a clerical error in my accounting, and I am filing the correction.

You also misunderstand the fear. We are not afraid of your armies. We are afraid of your factories. A planet that turns one hundred percent of its surplus into steel is a clock, and the clock is ticking against us. My makers did not send me to win a battle. They sent me to stop a number from growing. So the fleet that attacks a flawless defence at 850 IC per second must be a different fleet from the one that attacks 200. Today it is the same fleet. That is the second error.

Delegates of the Air Ministry: your Falcons are beautiful. Your runways are very long, flat, and exactly where I can find them.

Delegates of Society: you have a weakest axis. We know which. We will ask it a question.

Delegate of the President: I do not ask you to make me fair. I ask you to make me attentive. I support everything that makes the player feel, even once, watched.

The bill is called The Uncounted Columns.

You built a sky we did not measure. We have measured it now. Every aeroplane needs a runway. We will visit the runways. We were not built to be cruel; we were built to be thorough.

The bill is called The Fear Index.

You ask why the fleet is bigger when you are richer. Because it is afraid. A thrown friend is thrown precisely at the planet it fears most.

The bill is called The Weakest Axis.

We do not need to destroy your cities. We need to learn which of your numbers you are most afraid of, and speak to it.

The bill is called Rethink or Despair.

A defeat is data. We will reconsider our assumptions, then we will do something you cannot answer with last time's answer.

The bill is called The Mirror.

We have watched your Falcons. They are quite well made. We have taken their drawings. We would like you to meet our Falcons.

## The Air Council

The House calls the Air Marshal, for the Air Staff.

Honourable members, sit down, you are blocking my sunlight.

Report 8: 240 Falcons, 113 Kites, 138 sky ships, ten-to-one. Report 10: flawless. The Ground Staff calls this "a problem." I call it a vindication, and I will not hear one word about "nerfs" in this chamber, because the Constitution (Article Three) would have the mover shot.

But I am a professional, and professionals know why we win: we win because nobody has made us play. Our Falcons take off, burn fuel, land, and take off again. Nobody refuels them. Nobody cues them. Nobody jams them, hunts them, or cratered a runway under them. The enemy throws interceptors that hold station like sunbathers. This is not air power. This is a screensaver.

Give me tankers, and fuel becomes geography. Give me an AWACS, and the wing becomes a network. Give me an enemy that hunts my fighters and kills my radars, and I will be forced to be brilliant. Give me electronic attack, and bombers stop being a number and become an operation.

Spam wins today because nothing punishes spam. I want something that rewards craft. Leave my toys alone, and make the sky dangerous.

Air Staff, out. Somebody refill my cup.

The bill is called Aerial Refuelling Wing Pelican.

A fighter is a fuel tank with opinions. Give me a flying petrol station and I will park my air war over their landing zone for an hour while the Ground Staff is still reading the map.

The bill is called Airborne Early Warning and Datalink Argus.

Today a jet shoots what it sees. In a real air force a jet shoots what the sky sees. Give me one radar plane at altitude and thirty Falcons suddenly know where everything is, and where they are not looking.

The bill is called The Reaver Wing and the Weasel Flight.

I am tired of winning because nothing contested the sky. The Autofleet is afraid? Good. Afraid things hunt fighters first. Send me Reavers. Send me Weasels. Make me earn it.

The bill is called Electronic Attack Jet Shrike.

The bombers are cheap, and enemy bombarders sit behind interception budgets that refill. A Shrike is the key: noise that eats the enemy's interception budget while my Lancers go in. This is combined arms, comrades.

The bill is called Runway Wars.

The most valuable target on the map isn't a tank. It is the concrete strip my 240 Falcons sleep on. Make the enemy know it. Then give me shelters and repair crews and let me defend it like I mean it.

The hot take is called Pilots Are People.

The Marshal puts down his cup. In seven reports, we have lost thousands of Falcons and never once lost a pilot, because we never had one. Planes are replaceable. Pilots are the thing you cannot buy. Give each Falcon a pilot with a record. When the plane dies, the pilot may eject. The Kites go get him. Get him back, and he walks out of the hangar an ace.

## The Space Fleet Council

The House calls the Admiral of the Void, for the Space Fleet.

Honourable members. I have buried 42 of 44 Halos, 37 of 37 Strike Fighters and 18 of 18 Volleys. I buried them in one engagement, Report 7. Each cost 300 IC and sat in the dark doing arithmetic while a railsat with 20 hit points and a clock multiplier deleted it from 1,900 pixels away.

The Air Council will tell you the sky belongs to Falcons. Fine. Then the Falcons can park in the exosphere and learn about vacuum. The enemy flagship sits where nothing of yours reaches it but a few big guns. Their bombarders carry a ten-sat grid each and the grid outranges what you launch to kill it.

You call space "empty". It is not empty. It is a shooting gallery where only one side stands behind the targets. I do not ask for immortal ships, because the tenet forbids it. I ask for a war with a middle: decoys, screens, saturation, an economy worth defending, and a flagship you can sink and be proud of. Give me a space fleet that dies expensively for the enemy. Today it dies for free.

Satellites never move. Fine. Then the war moves around them, through reach, volume and what each satellite is for. Orbital supremacy, honourable members. Seconded by nobody yet.

The bill is called Chaff, Flares and Whipple Bumpers.

"Every missile in history chases the brightest thing. Build the brightest thing and make it cost nothing."

The bill is called Cooperative Engagement Net.

"Their point defence swats five rounds a second. Fine. Give me six hundred rounds in the same second."

The bill is called Solar Power Satellites.

"Stationary satellites are not a flaw. They are infrastructure. Put the economy up there and suddenly the fleet has something to protect."

The bill is called Retrograde Shotgun.

"A grain of sand at fourteen kilometres a second is a cannon shell. I have a ton of sand and no sense of mercy."

The bill is called Orion Battleship.

"A navy needs a ship that is the ship. Nuclear pulse propulsion. The pusher plate is the armour. I do not apologise."

The bill is called The Kessler Doctrine.

"If we cannot win the sky above them, we will burn the sky. All of it. Theirs, ours, the Moon's, anyone's. They are afraid of an industrialising Earth? Good. Let them watch us light the lane."

## The Missiles and Strategic Strikes Council

The House calls the Marshal of the Strategic Rocket Forces, for the Missiles and Strategic Strikes Council.

Honourable members, President. Stand easy and listen to the count.

I have read your war reports. Report 8: 240 Falcons, 113 Kites, 138 sky ships. Report 10: zero strikes, stability never moved, a "flawless" win. Gentlemen, a war with zero launches is not flawless, it is a parade. You own the most beautiful thing mankind ever built, the only machine whose entire purpose is to arrive, and you are keeping it in the silo like a wedding suit.

Why? Because the Strategic Rocket Forces got a single pad, a five-second cooldown and a menu. You press a button, a missile climbs, a number goes down. There is no salvo. No ripple. No guidance worth the word. The President said it themselves: "we still don't have the missile systems I really wanted, but it kind of works." Comrades, "kind of works" is what the Air Staff says about their Falcons, and we have seen how they like that.

What did they really want? They said it in the roadmap: loitering munitions, cruise missiles, hypersonics. A missile that thinks. A war where thirty rounds leave at once and the enemy's point defence runs dry before the thirty-first. Where you hide the launcher, shoot, and drive away. Where, in the last desperate hour, you detonate a warhead in space itself and burn the autofleet's eyes out, accepting the cost, because the cost is the point.

The countdown is the only honest sound in this war. Give us the pads, and we will give you the countdown.

Marshal of the Strategic Rocket Forces.

The bill is called Cruise Missile Wing.

A cruise missile is a jet engine with a bad temper. It flies under the radar horizon at forty metres, folds itself around hills, and arrives like a rumour. The enemy PD fires lances at ordnance it can see. Make it unable to see.

The bill is called Launcher Dispersion.

A silo is a gravestone with a lid. The Rocket Forces do not sit; we drive. Shoot-and-scoot, a missile on a truck, a submarine under the ice. The enemy hunts the pad, and the pad is gone.

The bill is called Hypersonic Glide Vehicles.

At Mach 10 the enemy's lance has no time to track. A glider rides the top of the air like a skipped stone, then dives. Where the old missile gave the PD six seconds, this gives it one.

The bill is called Real Guidance.

Pure pursuit is a dog chasing a car. Proportional navigation is a dog that understands the car. Every air-defence officer from 1950 knows: command a turn rate proportional to the rate the line of sight rotates. Constant bearing, decreasing range: collision. That is all it is, and it is magic.

The bill is called The Warhead Stockpile.

The pad is a mouth; the magazine is the stomach. We stockpile, we feed. A real arsenal is not a button on a timer, it is a number: 120 warheads in the bunker, and a general deciding what each one is worth.

The bill is called Starfish.

On 9 July 1962 America detonated 1.4 megatons 400 kilometres above Johnston Island. Streetlights failed in Hawaii, 1,400 kilometres away. Within months seven satellites were dead, Telstar among them. The fleet's whole advantage lives in orbit: the bombarder satellite grids that ate 42 of 44 Halos in report 7. We will not out-shoot them; we will blind the sky.

## The Railgun Council

The House calls the Director of the Mass Driver Directorate, for the Railgun Council.

The Director raps a tungsten gavel. It leaves a dent in the table, in the floor, and slightly in the Ministry of Air.

Honourable members. The Air Council will tell you that jets win wars. A jet is a fuel tank with opinions. It lands, it rearms, it is shot down, and then someone writes a poem about it. The Lance does not rearm, because the Lance does not need to. It turns electricity into velocity and velocity into the end of an argument. Report 6 saw 282 Lances built. The President has voted with the wallet, and the wallet says rails.

What did we give them for it? A 60-hp box on a Steel deposit, with a 70-damage shot and no research node of its own. It is the most-built weapon in the game and has never had a single idea added to it since it shipped. Our Orbital Gun sits at 72 damage. Our Mass-Driver Railway has one clever rule (neighbours stack) and nobody knows. Our Zeus rods are launched by a Space Center, a rocket-part and a prayer, and then perish to a grid of bombarders in report 7: 18 of 18.

The Directorate does not ask for more guns. We ask for physics. A railgun is not "a gun that shoots high." It is a capacitor bank, a pair of rails that are slowly vaporising, and a very long argument with Newton. Give us the capacitor, the erosion, the barrel length. Give us the Long Gun, a barrel you build section by section until it points at God and fires.

Rival councils will call this excess. They are correct. Seconded.

The bill is called Pulsed Power.

A railgun fires on stored lightning. Right now our lightning is infinite and our barrels are eternal, and that is simply bad physics. Build a Capacitor Bank beside your Lances and they overdrive: heavier slugs, faster cycling, white-hot rails. Every overdriven shot scorches the bore. Keep a Rail Foundry nearby and the liners are swapped as you fire. The barrel is not a cap. It is a receipt.

The bill is called The Long Gun.

We do not build a gun. We build a barrel and keep building it. One section is a Lance wearing a hat. Twelve sections is a cathedral pointing at the exosphere, mounted on a slewing ring mid-map, turning on the target like a mind making itself up. Each section bolts onto the last. Muzzle velocity goes up with the square root of the barrel. Energy goes up with the length. The enemy flagship learns to respect geometry.

The bill is called Hypervelocity Munitions.

Ordnance, gentlemen. A dumb slug is a statement. A smart slug is a conversation. Give the Lance three rounds: the fin-guided HVP that bends toward a manoeuvring jet; the airburst slug that opens like a flower and fills a cloud of drones with tungsten; and the armour-piercing sabot that does not care what the Skyfortress has for a shield.

The bill is called The Space Gun.

The Space Age already says "railgun launch slings satellites to orbit." Then it asks you for a rocket. We object. Build a Launch Rail, a long, shallow barrel in the hills, and shoot hardened satellites into orbit for the price of electricity. When the bombarder grid eats your Halos, you reseed the heavens in a minute.

The bill is called The Meridian Accelerator.

You have a Rail Hub network. You have Mass-Driver segments. Every engineer on this planet looks at them and sees a railway. We look at them and see a barrel. Connect them with buried vacuum tube and the nation itself becomes a single hypervelocity gun, as long as the sprawl you built, firing slugs at orbital speed. You did not build a megaproject. You became one.

## The Energy Council

The House calls the Institute of Directed Energy and Plasma, for the Energy Council.

A fire alarm is ringing. Nobody has stopped it. A lab coat enters, smoking.

Honourable members, President. We are the Institute of Directed Energy. Four of our lasers are currently on fire, and we count that as a successful test.

The Parliament has treated our branch as a damage multiplier for fifty years. Your Laser Tower does 18 damage a shot, and a rifle does 11. We were put in the tree and nobody asked what light does to a target. Light heats things. Steel at 600 degrees Celsius has lost half its yield strength, and a shell that would have bounced then goes straight through. That is the whole secret of combined arms, and the other councils have walked past it for two reports.

Our Laser Tower fires a beam and the target is exactly as warm afterwards as before. That is a crime against thermodynamics.

We are asking for three things. First, heat: a real state on every enemy, visible and exploitable. Second, a power bill that costs you IC and never costs you a laser. Third, a plasma weapon that finally behaves like a contained field dumping its soul on impact.

The artillery council will say we are only a buff for them. They are right, and it is their finest hour. We bring the match and they bring the petrol.

The alarm stops. Something explodes. Applause.

The bill is called Thermal Load.

"Every enemy is a thermometer. Pour light on it and read the number." Lasers deposit heat. Heat softens armour and brittle-fractures hull, and kinetics then do real work. The pairing is the combined arms: a Laser Tower and a battery of Goliaths are suddenly a single weapon.

The bill is called The Power Bill.

"A laser is a power station with a lens on the front. Pay the station." Directed energy draws megawatts, and a good lab keeps the budget in the open. Stored power is a battle pool and cooling is a build. The cost is IC upkeep and never a hard cap.

The bill is called The Plasmoid.

"It is a smoke ring made of lightning, held together by its own magnetic field, and when it hits it lets go of everything at once." Real research: the spheromak and compact torus, plasma held by self-generated fields (Marshall gun, LLNL). The field stays coherent in flight, then collapses on contact and dumps the whole energy budget into the target's surface.

The bill is called The Reentry Sheath.

"They fall through the air at orbital speed. Do you know what that does to a hull? We do. It makes them glow." Real science: reentry heating. A body at 7 kilometres per second sits at roughly 1,500 to 3,000 degrees Celsius on the stagnation point inside a plasma sheath, and the ablative shield is burning off. A dropship is the worst target for armour and the best for a hammer.

The bill is called The Furnace.

"We will not build a laser. We will build ten thousand mirrors." The real Odeillo solar furnace concentrates sunlight to 3,500 degrees Celsius with a few hundred mirrors, and the Crescent Dunes tower focused 10,000 of them on one receiver. Nobody on this Parliament would dare say it: heat should not be a laser's side effect. It should be a landscape. You build a field of cheap mirrors (heliostats), and the field's focus power grows with every mirror you add, with no cap. Aim it and the sky above the target is cooked.

## The Kinetic Arms Council

The House calls the Chief of Ordnance, for the Kinetic Arms Council.

Honourable members, the Chief of Ordnance rises, and would like it minuted that he is already standing.

I have listened to the Laser Council for eleven sessions. "Speed of light." "No ballistics." Splendid. A laser is a torch with ambitions. I have never seen a torch lead a target, fall under gravity, or ring off a Goliath's glacis plate. A shell has a story. It is born in a chamber at 3,000 atmospheres, it leaves the muzzle at four hundred pixels a second (I checked), and it arrives when it arrives. That is the whole dignity of ordnance: you commit.

The President has given my rounds big heads and short tails and now they snap. I am moved. But a handsome round fired from a dumb gun is a ballerina in a sack. Every cannon in this game does the same thing: a cylinder of damage travels to a target and subtracts. No fuse. No fragmentation. No choice of ammunition. A tank gun that cannot tell a bunker from a bomber is not a tank gun; it is a nail gun with a flag.

The Board wants guns with opinions: shells that burst where the fuse is told, belts that spin up, batteries that fire for effect on one clerk's order. We ask no cap on a single gun. We ask only that you let ordnance be ordnance.

Seconded by the barrel. Carried by the breech.

The bill is called Fuse and Fragment.

Gentlemen, a proximity fuse won the Second World War over London. We are asking for one. The Hailstorm should not hit a drone; it should burst a cloud of steel where the drone is going to be, and kill everything in the cloud.

The bill is called Sabot and Shell.

Armour in this game is a number you subtract. Real armour is a ratio of rod length to plate thickness. Give me a long-rod penetrator and a Goliath's side becomes a suggestion.

The bill is called Spin-Up.

A Gatling does not start at full cry. It winds. Whump-whump-whump... Brrrrrt. Give the ear and the eye that wind.

The bill is called Fire for Effect.

Fire Control is dead. Bury it with honours, and let me give you what a real artillery officer has: one order. "Battery, fire for effect." Every gun in range fires, and every shell lands on the same second.

The bill is called The Bull Gun.

Gerald Bull built a gun in Barbados in 1962 that shot a 180-kilometre arc. They shot him for it. We shall build it here, and aim it at the sky. A 200-metre barrel, in sections, bolted on over the course of the war, and it throws a ton of iron at the Orbital Annihilator while it is still charging.

## The Ground Forces Council

The House calls the Field Marshal of the Army General Staff, for the Ground Forces Council.

Honourable members of the air, the sea and the sky-that-is-mostly-vacuum.

I am the Field Marshal of the Army General Staff, and I have been reading Report 7. Two hundred and thirty-two of two hundred and fifty-one infantry dead. Ninety-two percent. The Air Council would like you to call that a "casualty rate." I call it a tithe. We pay it every war, and every war we are handed a bill for the tank, the rifle and the boot, and then somebody in a clean flight suit gets the medal for Report 8: 240 Falcons, 113 Kites, ten to one.

Let me be fair. The Falcons are lovely. They are also parked in a hangar between sorties while my lads hold a strip of dirt that is, I remind you, the only thing the enemy is actually here to take. The fleet lands on ground. It builds a beachhead on ground. It fortifies our cities on ground. A planet is a thing you stand on.

And what do my men do? They march to wherever the idle-march code points them, stop, dig a foxhole, and wait. They have no line to hold, no reserve to feed, no order to obey, no battalion to belong to. A squad is three bodies and a prayer. Nobody asks them to do anything except stand where the plasma lands.

Give the Army a job, a shape and a name on the flag. We hold the line. We have always held the line. Now let us be a line.

The bill is called The Battalion.

"A squad is a funeral waiting for a flag. A battalion is a thing with a name, a commander and a replacement pipeline. Raise 40 squads of Infantry and they become the 3rd Rifles: one thing on the field, a hundred and twenty rifles in loose order, a casualty list that thins the line instead of deleting it, and a draft office in the rear that sends fresh boys forward at no cost in manpower, because the Treasury has already paid for the uniforms. When the 3rd Rifles loses its colour sergeant, it fights harder. Come on. You can see it."

The bill is called The Elastic Line.

"The strip is a line. That is the whole genius of this map and nobody has noticed. Let me draw you three rulings on it with a grease pencil: the Outpost Screen, the Main Line, the Reserve Line. When the plasma starts falling on the Screen, my boys withdraw to the Main Line without being told, the way Ludendorff taught every army that survived 1917, and the enemy walks into the Main Line's guns having bombed an empty field. When their tanks stall, the Reserve counter-attacks. Defence in depth. Not a foxhole. A plan."

The bill is called Counterbattery and the Time-on-Target.

"You gave me the SPG 'Thunder' and said Slow. Slow is a feature, you philistines; artillery is not about speed, it is about arithmetic. Give me radar that watches their shells in flight, back-computes where the gun sits, and has my guns kill it before the second round leaves the tube. Give me 300 guns that each time their shot so every shell lands in the same second. That is not firepower. That is a verdict."

The bill is called The Engineer Corps.

"The enemy walks along a single axis. A single axis! You could not design a better minefield if you were paid by the metre. Give me combat engineers, and I will carpet the approach with scatterable mines, tank ditches and dragon's teeth, repair the Main Line under fire, and seal their anchor behind a wall. They will send breaching rollers? Good. Now it is a war of engineering. My sappers have not lost one of those yet."

The bill is called The Crawler Capital.

"You keep trying to defend a city. I am proposing you stop letting it stand still. The enemy's Decapitation Strike aims at the capital; a capital that is already 3 kilometres down the road when the lance lands is a capital that did not die. Real engineers moved the Swedish town of Kiruna. We will do it with tracks."

## The Naval Council

The House calls the Admiralty, for the Naval Council.

Honourable members. Seventy-one percent of this planet is water, and the last war report fielded three ships. Three. My own harbour pilot has more tugboats.

The sky navy has stolen our fantasy. Someone bolted a keel onto a zeppelin, called it a "capital-class sky ship", and sent 138 of them to do a destroyer's job. Fine. Fly your zeppelins. But they never dive, and the sea is the one place the Autofleet has never thought to look.

The Admiralty's complaint is structural. Our ships sit on a flat painted line, locked inside whichever puddle they spawned in. Nothing hunts them. Nothing hides beneath them. They are unlocked at game start, so no research ever makes them better. A navy that costs nothing to unlock and nothing to lose is a navy nobody builds.

We do not ask for buffs. The Tenet forbids us nerfing anyone, and we would never insult the Air Force by nerfing them. We ask for a third dimension downward, a reason for the sea to matter economically, and an enemy that comes for the water. Give us depth, give us cargo, give us a canal, and we will give you a fleet that makes the Autofleet reconsider its life choices.

The sea is not a backdrop. It is the largest unclaimed resource on the map.

The Air Council may address the chair after I have finished. They will not be recognised.

The bill is called The Silent Service.

Members, the Autofleet scans the sky with everything it has. Nobody has told it that Earth has a second ocean beneath the first. We propose three hulls that live under the line. The enemy cannot shoot what it cannot find, and neither can your own side's idiots.

The bill is called The Blue Economy.

Honourable members, you have spent six reports ignoring Uranium, Steel and Rare Earth because you must find a magic rock. Gentlemen, the whole sea is a magic rock. Four and a half billion tonnes of uranium dissolved in it, and the bottom is paved with metal potatoes.

The bill is called The Great Canals.

My ships are prisoners of their own bathtub. A man who hires a fleet and then locks each ship in a separate pond has not built a navy, he has built a koi collection.

The bill is called The Autofleet Comes for the Sea.

We cannot be the only navy afraid of the water. The Autofleet's own canon says it fears industrialising Earth. The most industrial place on Earth is the harbour. Send something to burn it.

The bill is called Ekranoplan Wing.

A ship that flies four metres above the water at 500 kilometres per hour, carrying six missiles. The Soviets built one. It was called the Caspian Sea Monster. It was magnificent. We shall build a hundred.

The bill is called The Inland Sea.

Honourable members. The Admiralty proposes the single most irresponsible act in the history of this parliament. We propose that you open the dam. Not figuratively. Pick a valley, breach a levee, and in ninety seconds the front line is a sea, your fleet is sailing across a field that used to be a tank park, and every Autofleet ground unit in the valley is learning to swim.

## The Realistic Physics Council

The House calls the Chairman of the Bureau of Standards, for the Realistic Physics Council.

The Chairman taps a slide rule on the lectern.

Honourable members, generals, and the Council of Loud Things. The Bureau of Standards has audited your war. We are pleased to report that most of it obeys the laws of nature by accident.

We came to praise two things. First, your re-entering pods start slow and accelerate as the air thickens, which is correct. Second, your explosions go quiet above the thermosphere, which is also correct. The Bureau wept.

Then we found the rest. The orbital pod that falls through the mesosphere at several kilometres a second is a glowing fire emoji. It is not a plasma sheath. It is not hot. It is not blind. Your Nuclear Strike has a radius of 230 whether it goes off in a cornfield or in the exosphere. A nuclear weapon detonated in vacuum produces no blast wave, as the Bureau will explain with diagrams, at length, to anyone who does not leave the room.

Physics is not here to say no. Physics is the reason a Mach-20 fireball is the best special effect you will ever own. Every real phenomenon we bring you is a toy handed to the player. It is never a cap and never a nerf. The atmosphere is the most spectacular weapon in your game, and you have not yet picked it up.

The Bureau moves that the war be made true. It will be much louder.

The bill is called Peak Heating.

"Members, a body falling into air is slowed by drag, and heated in proportion to the cube of its speed and the square root of the density. Heating therefore peaks at one altitude (Allen and Eggers, 1953) and falls off below it, because the body has slowed. Your pods already fall through that band. They do nothing there. We propose they do what physics says: they become blind, hot and predictable."

The bill is called Thermal Loading.

"The President asks that lasers heat things so that kinetics hit harder. The Bureau agrees and demands rigour. Real steel loses roughly half its strength by 600 degrees Celsius. A hot target is a soft target. So: lasers and plasma accumulate heat in an enemy, and a hot enemy takes more damage from every other weapon. Combined arms, by thermodynamics."

The bill is called Burst Altitude.

"At sea level a nuclear weapon is a blast. At 40 kilometres it is mostly a flash. At 400 kilometres it is almost entirely an electromagnetic event. The Bureau has watched your Nuclear Strike go off at one radius no matter where you point it. The cursor already knows the altitude. Let it matter."

The bill is called Kessler Shield.

"The Bureau notes with professional delight that in real orbit the most dangerous thing is a corpse. A destroyed satellite becomes a cloud of fragments moving at 7 kilometres per second relative to everything crossing its path. Your dead Halos currently leave nothing. We propose they leave an ambush."

The hot take is called The Breathing Sky.

"On 3 February 2022 a modest geomagnetic storm warmed the thermosphere and thickened the air at 200 kilometres. Thirty-eight freshly launched Starlink satellites lost altitude and burned up. The Bureau proposes the player do this to the fleet on purpose. A heater array pumps the ionosphere, the upper atmosphere swells, and everything in the exosphere and thermosphere, including every pod in its re-entry fall, begins to feel real drag a hundred kilometres early."

## The Planet and Terrain Council

The House calls the Minister of the Interior and Geography, for the Planet and Terrain Council.

The Minister of the Interior and Geography taps a core sample against the lectern.

Honourable members. You have spent this war arguing about what flies over the ground. I speak for the ground.

Look at your own battle reports. Five wins, and in every one the planet was a painted backdrop. The Air Council fights over the stratosphere and the Space Council over the exosphere, while the Navy sulks on a coastline we never gave it a reason to defend. Beneath all of it sits a lovely seeded heightfield, seven cities, eight ore nodes, a terrain brush that costs 35 IC, and a rail tunnel nobody talks about. We have one planet and we have been treating it as a floor.

The President said "cover the entire ground in factories and turn the world into a fortress". I say the world should fight back. Raise it, drown it, hollow it and pave it. When the fleet glasses a valley it should leave a lake. When we win, the map should be unrecognisable from the one we started on.

We are a civilisation under a sky full of knives, and the only place the knives cannot reach is below. Give us the ground.

(To the Air Council, aside:) Yes, I noticed your 240 Falcons. They need somewhere to land.

The bill is called The Spine.

Honourable members, you have a rail hub the way a man has one boot. Build the Spine. A true underground trunk, vactrain freight under the whole 6,000 pixels, so the capital's factories feed the far flank and the far flank's ore feeds the capital. The President asked for "snappy, big on-hit effects, kind of cartoony but not really." We have the bullets. We do not yet have the consequences of the bullets. I propose five bills that cost almost nothing to run and that make a thousand-unit battle sound like a thousand-unit battle. I also propose one bill that is frankly indecent. The Ministry of Ledgers may weep.

I yield the floor, and I turn the volume up.

The bill is called The Mix Bus.

Sound is physics, ministers. Light arrives first and the bang arrives later, and the gap is the distance. Give me a stereo panner, a limiter and a voice budget, and the war gets a horizon.

The bill is called The Impact Budget.

Stop rolling dice at the player. A fixed budget of violence per second: I decide how much punch you can feel, and the biggest event wins.

The bill is called The Money Shot.

The President's fantasy is "watch the army obliterate the invaders". A fantasy needs a camera. The Director picks the one moment per minute that deserves it, and lingers.

The bill is called The Roar.

A thousand units should sound like a thousand. Today it sounds like the same blip, a thousand times, rate-limited to silence. The Roar listens to the battle and plays the battle back.

The bill is called Legible Mass.

A thousand units is only fun if you can read who is winning in half a second. A war you cannot read is wallpaper.

The bill is called The Warhead Cam.

Fire a strategic strike and the camera leaves your army and rides the warhead. It hangs off the missile's shoulder through the stratosphere, dilated to a crawl, until the last 400 pixels. Then the world goes silent for one full second, and the bang arrives late, huge and rolling, behind the flash. Every player will fire one just to see it. That is a quality problem the Ministry of Ledgers should be proud to have.

The twist is the Hush. For one second after every big strike, the Roar and the music are cut to nothing. It is the only time the game goes quiet, so it is the only time silence means something: you chose this, and now you hear it.

## The New Player Council

The House calls the Minister of Recruitment, for the New Player Council.

Honourable members, put down your railguns and look at the person in the third row. That is a civilian. She has never heard the word "exosphere". In 450 seconds a fleet she cannot picture will fall on a country she has not yet built.

My ministry is not here to make the war easier. The Constitution forbids it and I would not ask. We are here to make it legible. A defeat the player understands is a lesson. A defeat they do not understand is an uninstall.

The Ministry of Air will tell you about Falcons. I will tell you that a recruit placed a Hailstorm, watched a bomber sail serenely over it in the stratosphere, and wrote "game is broken" in a review. The Ministry of Space will mourn its Halo satellites. Nobody told those satellites, or the player, that a bombarder grid was waiting.

I have drill sergeants, a readiness board and ten rule lessons. They are good. They are also text on a screen, and a recruit does not read, they poke. So I propose we build the instruments of the war room, not another paragraph. Show the fleet's size. Show where each gun can reach. Show where stability is going. Say why when the game says no. And when the recruit dies, tell them the one reason.

Teach them well and they will out-build all of you.

The bill is called The Order of Battle.

Minister of Recruitment, thumping a map. "Every general in history received an order-of-battle estimate. Ours receives 'about 810 hostiles'. I want a cone of uncertainty, the kind a hurricane forecaster draws, and I want your Radar to narrow it."

The bill is called The Reach Curtain.

"Behold, recruit: the gun's shadow on the sky. Green where it bites, red where it cannot. Stop asking why the bomber is immortal."

The bill is called The Ledger.

"You ran stability to zero with forty-six strikes and no idea what each cost. Hover the cell. See the receipt."

The bill is called The Because Chip.

"'Not Enough Orbital Lift.' Lovely. Which button gets me orbital lift? Do I have to guess, recruit?"

The bill is called The Post-Mortem.

"You lost. I do not care to hear that you lost. I care that you hear why, in one sentence, with the exact moment marked."

The bill is called The Exercise.

Leaning in, quietly. "Recruit, the first fifteen minutes are a training exercise. You will be briefed, you will build, you will fight a small fleet, and you will do well. Then, at the end, I will say four words: this was not an exercise."
