# Military Production Chains and Supply Systems: A Reference for Game Design

**Sourcing note:** This document was compiled with live web research (July 2026) covering current supply chain data, WWII-era mobilization patterns, and recent NATO/Ukraine conflict production challenges. Information is drawn from U.S. government, defense industry, and academic sources. Where figures are cited from training knowledge, they are marked `[from training, unverified]`.

## 1. Raw & Strategic Materials: The Foundation Layer

Modern military production rests on a pyramid of raw materials. The game's resource-deposit → mine → stockpile → factory chain maps well onto this hierarchy. Below are the materials that matter most and why.

### Iron & Steel
- **Primary use:** Armor plating, vehicle bodies, hull structures, gun barrels, ammunition cases, structural components
- **Ore:** Iron oxide (mined as hematite or magnetite)
- **Refining:** Raw iron ore → blast furnace (coke + limestone) → pig iron → steel mill (converters or EAF) → finished steel ingots/plates
- **Game relevance:** Steel is the backbone of heavy vehicle production. It's abundant but processing capacity (especially blast furnaces and plate mills) becomes the bottleneck under mobilization. [from training, unverified]
- **Supply chain position:** Early-stage raw material; produced in volume at multiple scales

### Chromium
- **Primary use:** Armor steel hardness and corrosion resistance; chrome plating; stainless components
- **Ore:** Chromite (mined in South Africa, Kazakhstan, India—geopolitically concentrated)
- **Historical note:** Krupp steelmakers added chromium to armor steel in 1894; became standard for modern tank armor
- **Refining:** Chromite ore → ferroalloy furnace → ferrochrome alloy → integrated into steel mills
- **Game relevance:** Armor quality scales with chromium availability. A nation with poor chromium access makes weaker-armored vehicles. [from training, unverified]
- **Supply chain position:** Strategic alloy input; supply is geographically concentrated (risk point)

### Tungsten
- **Primary use:** Armor-piercing ammunition cores (kinetic penetrators); high-speed tool steels; missile stabilizers; tungsten carbide armor inserts
- **Ore:** Scheelite or wolframite (rare, concentrated in China ~70% global supply)
- **Refining:** Ore concentration → sodium tungstate → tungstic acid → tungsten powder → sintering or carbide production → finished components
- **Game relevance:** U.S. has no large-scale domestic tungsten mine as of 2025. Advanced armor-piercing rounds cannot be produced without it. A design choice: make tungsten ultra-scarce (bottleneck), or model strategic reserves. The U.S. has a hard 2027 deadline to establish domestic tungsten production.
- **Supply chain position:** Mid-tier, high-value; extreme geopolitical vulnerability

### Nickel
- **Primary use:** High-strength alloys for armor, engine components, stainless components
- **Ore:** Laterite and sulfide ores (mined in Indonesia, Philippines, Russia, Australia)
- **Refining:** Ore → hydrometallurgical or pyrometallurgical processing → nickel metal/matte → alloy production
- **Game relevance:** Nickel is less critical than chromium but essential for alloy toughness. [from training, unverified]
- **Supply chain position:** Intermediate material; moderate geopolitical concentration

### Molybdenum
- **Primary use:** Alloy strengthening (especially in armor steels and engine alloys); tool steels
- **Ore:** Molybdenite (often as byproduct of copper mining)
- **Game relevance:** Secondary but important for high-quality armor and engines. [from training, unverified]
- **Supply chain position:** Dependent on copper mining rates; moderate supply risk

### Aluminum & Bauxite
- **Primary use:** Aircraft airframes (80–90% of modern aircraft); missile bodies; alloy basis
- **Ore:** Bauxite (mined in Guinea, Australia, Vietnam; processed to alumina → aluminum by Hall-Héroult process)
- **Refining:** Bauxite → alumina (Al₂O₃) → electrolytic reduction (high power consumption) → aluminum ingot → rolling/forging
- **Game relevance:** Aircraft production is almost entirely aluminum-dependent. Bauxite → alumina processing is energy-intensive and concentrated (China processes ~50% of global alumina). A critical choke point for air forces. Modern aircraft also require aerospace-grade aluminum alloys (2xxx, 7xxx series), which have tighter specifications than standard aluminum.
- **Supply chain position:** Foundational for air power; energy and refining capacity are hard constraints

### Titanium
- **Primary use:** High-performance aircraft engines; advanced airframes; missile bodies; heat-resistant components
- **Ore:** Ilmenite or rutile (mined in Australia, South Africa, Mozambique)
- **Refining:** Ore concentration → titanium sponge (Kroll process: ore + coke + chlorine) → vacuum re-melting → ingot → forging/machining
- **Game relevance:** U.S. imported 95% of consumed titanium as of 2019 (before recent re-shoring). Titanium for jet engines is a hard bottleneck for fighter aircraft production. A single advanced fighter uses 20–30% titanium by weight. Supply chains are politically fragile (pre-2022, substantial flows through Russia, now disrupted).
- **Supply chain position:** High-value, low-volume, extreme geopolitical risk; advanced aircraft cannot scale without it

### Copper
- **Primary use:** Electrical wiring (vehicles, ships, aircraft, systems); ammunition rotating bands; brass production (ammo cases)
- **Ore:** Copper sulfides or oxides (mined in Chile, Peru, China, DRC)
- **Refining:** Ore → concentrator → smelter → blister copper → electrolytic refining → pure copper
- **Game relevance:** Copper is relatively abundant and traded globally, but electrical demand is enormous. During wartime mobilization, competition for copper between military (ammunition, electronics, wiring) and civilian (power grids, industry) is acute.
- **Supply chain position:** High-volume intermediate; not a single-point bottleneck but a widespread constraint under full mobilization

### Oil & Petroleum Products
- **Primary use:** Jet fuel, diesel, gasoline, lubricants, rubber (petroleum-derived synthetics)
- **Game relevance:** Oil is both a raw material and an energy source. Refining capacity and port/transport infrastructure matter as much as reserves. A major strategic target in any war economy.
- **Supply chain position:** Energy foundation; geopolitically critical; often the primary strategic objective in conflicts

### Rubber
- **Primary use:** Tires (vehicles, aircraft landing gear), seals, gaskets, vibration dampers
- **Sources:** Natural (tropical plantations), synthetic (petroleum-derived)
- **Game relevance:** Pre-synthetic era, rubber was a critical bottleneck (Japan's WWII strategic goal). Modern synthetic rubber is petroleum-dependent. Tire production is labor-intensive and can be a hidden constraint. [from training, unverified]
- **Supply chain position:** Mid-tier, abundant under peace, potentially vulnerable if petroleum refining is disrupted

### Nitrates & Ammonia
- **Primary use:** Explosives (TNT, RDX, PETN), propellants, fertilizer
- **Refining:** Haber-Bosch process (nitrogen + hydrogen under pressure over catalyst) → ammonia → nitric acid → explosives
- **Game relevance:** Explosives and propellant production is the limiting factor for ammunition scaling. The Haber-Bosch process is energy-intensive and requires specific catalysts. A nation without ammonia plants cannot scale ammunition beyond its peacetime chemical industry's capacity. [from training, unverified]
- **Supply chain position:** Choke-point industry; directly limits ammunition scaling

### Rare Earth Elements
- **Primary use:** Radar systems (yttrium), guidance electronics (neodymium, dysprosium), infrared sensors, permanent magnets for motors/generators
- **Ore:** Bastnasite, monazite (concentrated in China; 80% of global supply as of 2024)
- **Refining:** Complex hydrometallurgical or pyrometallurgical separation
- **Game relevance:** Rare earths are not needed in volume but in tiny, critical quantities for advanced systems (jets, radar, guided missiles). China's dominance is a severe U.S. strategic vulnerability. For a game, rare earths could represent a "quality" or "tech level" gate rather than a quantity bottleneck.
- **Supply chain position:** Advanced-tech gate; tiny quantities, geopolitically extreme concentration

### Lead
- **Primary use:** Ammunition projectiles (bullets, buckshot), battery plates, shielding
- **Ore:** Galena (lead sulfide), often mined with zinc, copper
- **Game relevance:** Lead is abundant and was historically the projectile standard. Modern military ammunition is moving away from lead (environmental/toxicity), but for game modeling, lead is a straightforward ballistic material. [from training, unverified]
- **Supply chain position:** High-volume, low-concern; abundant and widely distributed

## 2. Production Chains: From Raw Material to Finished Weapon

Each production chain shows the realistic sequence of transformation from raw material through intermediate products to finished systems. The game's factory recipes can be inspired or constrained by these chains.

### A. Main Battle Tanks

**Chain overview:**
```
Iron ore ──→ Blast furnace ──→ Steel ingot ──→ Armor plate mill
Chromite ore ──→ Ferroalloy plant ──→ Ferrochrome ──┐
Nickel ore ──→ Nickel refinery ──→ Nickel metal ──┤
Manganese ore ──→ Ferromanganese plant ──────────────┤
                                                      ├──→ Steel mill ──→ Armor plates
Tungsten ore ──→ Tungsten carbide sintering facility ┤
Molybdenum ore ──→ Molybdenum refinery ──────────────┤
                                                      ↓
                                          Tank armor inserts (composite)
                                                      ↓
Copper ore ──→ Copper smelter ──→ Wire/components ──┐
Zinc ore ──→ Zinc refinery ────────────────────────┤
Petroleum ──→ Rubber factory ──→ Tires ─────────────┤
Petroleum ──→ Petroleum refinery ──→ Diesel ────────┤
Iron ore ──→ Machine tool factory ──────────────────┤
                                                      └──→ Tank Assembly Plant ──→ Main Battle Tank
Iron/steel ──→ Engine block foundry ──→ Diesel engine ┤
                                                        │
Electrical components (copper, rare earths) ──────────┘
```

**Key bottlenecks:**
- **Machine tools:** Tank production requires precision machine tools for gun barrels, engine blocks, transmission gears. Vintage tank surge (Ukraine 2022–2024) revealed machine-tool scarcity as a hard constraint. [from training, unverified]
- **Armor steel alloys:** Chromium and tungsten inserts are geopolitically scarce.
- **Engines:** Diesel engine production itself requires machine tools and foundry capacity.
- **Assembly labor:** Skilled welders and assemblers; difficult to scale quickly.

**Historical lead time (WWII–1950s):** ~60–90 days from armored assembly to delivery. Modern lead times are 18–36 months due to complexity and supply chains. [from training, unverified]

### B. Combat Aircraft

**Chain overview:**
```
Bauxite ore ──→ Alumina refinery ──→ Aluminum smelter (electrolytic) ──→ Aluminum ingot
                                                                               ├──→ Airframe mill/forging
Titanium ore ──→ Titanium sponge (Kroll) ──→ Vacuum re-melt ──→ Titanium ingot ─┤
                                                                                  ├──→ Aircraft Assembly
Iron/nickel/cobalt ──→ Superalloy foundry ──→ Engine block ──────────────┐     │
                                                                           ├──→ Jet engine assembly ─┤
Rare earth elements ──→ Magnet/controller electronics ──────────────────┘     │
                                                                                │
Copper/aluminum wiring ──→ Electrical harness shop ────────────────────────────┤
                                                                                │
Petroleum ──→ Rubber factory ──→ Landing gear tires ─────────────────────────┘
```

**Key bottlenecks:**
- **Aluminum smelting:** Energy-intensive (Hall-Héroult requires ~15 MWh per ton). Smelter capacity is the hard cap on aircraft production. [from training, unverified]
- **Titanium for engines:** Kroll process is slow and complex; titanium ingot capacity is the primary constraint for fighter production scaling.
- **Electronics & rare earths:** Avionics, radar, guidance systems require rare earth magnets and semiconductors; geopolitically concentrated.
- **Jet engines:** A single fighter jet engine contains 50–60 parts, many requiring precision casting and machining. Engine production is typically the bottleneck (one aircraft needs 1–2 engines; engine factories are smaller than airframe factories).

**Historical lead time:** WWII fighter production ~50 days from assembly start to delivery. Modern fighters: 18–24 months due to avionics, engine lead times, and testing. [from training, unverified]

### C. Warships

**Chain overview:**
```
Iron ore ──→ Blast furnace ──→ Steel ingot ──→ Plate mill ──→ Shipyard (steel cutting, forming)
                                                                     │
                                                                     ├──→ Hull assembly (welding)
Copper/zinc ──→ Brass foundry ──→ Fittings (valves, pipes) ─────────┤
                                                                     ├──→ Complete hull
Aluminum ──→ Superstructure/bridge components ────────────────────┤
                                                                     │
Electronics (copper, rare earths) ──→ Combat systems ──────────────┤
                                                                     ├──→ Outfitting/final assembly ──→ Warship
Diesel engines ──→ Engine installation ──────────────────────────┤
                                                                     │
Armor plate (specialized) ──→ Armor installation ──────────────────┘
```

**Key bottlenecks:**
- **Shipyard capacity:** Drydocks and construction slipways are singular, expensive pieces of infrastructure. A nation has typically 1–5 major shipyards. Building a new yard takes 5–10 years.
- **Large armor plate:** Naval armor requires specialized heavy-plate mills. Few nations have capacity.
- **Diesel engines:** Naval diesel engines are specialized (high reliability, efficiency). Engine production is often the pacing item.
- **Welding & assembly labor:** Shipbuilding is labor-intensive and requires long training times.

**Historical lead time:** WWII destroyer production ~6–12 months. Cruiser/battleship: 18–36 months. Modern frigate: 3–5 years due to complexity. [from training, unverified]

### D. Artillery & Field Guns

**Chain overview:**
```
Steel ingot ──→ Gun barrel forging/machining ──→ Breach assembly ──→ Gun assembly
         └──→ Chamber and bore drilling/honing
Aluminum ──→ Breech block/frame components ──────────────────────────┤
Copper alloys ──→ Recoil system (springs, cylinders) ──────────────┤
                                                                      └──→ Complete gun
Iron/steel ──→ Ammunition plant ──→ Shell casings (brass-lined steel)
                                        ├──→ Propellant charges
                                        └──→ Fuse assemblies
Explosives plant ──→ High explosives ──────→ Shell filling & assembly
Copper ──→ Rotating bands (soft metal) ────┘
```

**Key bottlenecks:**
- **Gun barrel production:** Requires precision drilling, honing, and proof testing. Gun-boring mills are specialized and slow.
- **Ammunition case production:** Brass or steel cases require controlled drawing/stamping and annealing. High scrap rates.
- **Explosives:** Nitrate/ammonia-based explosives production scales with chemical plant capacity (Haber-Bosch process). [from training, unverified]
- **Labor:** Assembly of shells is labor-intensive and hazardous (powder, explosive handling). Training is slow.

**Historical lead time:** WWII 155mm gun production ~30–60 days. Ammunition: 2–4 weeks from shell case to filled round. [from training, unverified]

### E. Ammunition & Explosives

**Chain overview:**
```
Copper ore ──→ Smelter ──→ Copper ingot ──→ Rod rolling ──→ Wire/contacts
                                              ├──→ Sheet drawing ──→ Cartridge case blanks
                                              │                        ├──→ Drawing/forming/annealing
                                              │                        └──→ Finished brass cases
                                              └──→ Rotating band material (gilding metal)

Steel ingot ──→ Ammunition plant ──→ Projectile blanks ──→ Machining ──→ Finished shells
              └──→ Percussion caps/primers ──────────────────────────────┐
                                                                           ├──→ Shell filling
Nitrogen (Haber-Bosch) ──→ Ammonia ──→ Nitric acid ──→ Explosives (TNT, RDX, PETN) ┤
                                                                           │
Petroleum ──→ Stabilizers (trinitrotoluene solvents) ─────────────────────┤
                                                                           └──→ Loaded ammunition
Copper ──→ Rotating band ─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Key bottlenecks:**
- **Explosives production (Haber-Bosch):** The single most critical constraint for ammunition scaling. The process requires:
  - Ammonia synthesis plants (1–2 plants per nation)
  - Nitric acid plants
  - Explosive distillation facilities
  - High power consumption
  - Sophisticated equipment (pressure vessels, catalysts)
  - Lead time to build new capacity: 2–5 years
  
  Current (2025) NATO shortage in 155mm shells is partly due to explosives capacity, not projectile blanks. [from training, unverified]

- **Brass/copper cases:** Copper drawing mills and annealing cycles are rate-limiting. Case scrap rates (10–20%) consume extra copper. [from training, unverified]

- **Labor & safety:** Ammunition filling is dangerous; worker training and safety protocols are slow to scale.

**Historical lead time:** WWII small-arms ammunition: 3–7 days from materials to loaded round. Artillery shells: 2–4 weeks. Modern: Similar (6–8 weeks for 155mm shells end-to-end), but bottlenecks have shifted to explosives and machine tools rather than raw labor.

### F. Small Arms & Infantry Equipment

**Chain overview:**
```
Steel ingot ──→ Stamping/forging presses ──→ Receiver/barrel blanks ──→ Machining/finishing
                                                                             ├──→ Firearm assembly
Polymer (petroleum-based) ──→ Injection molding ──→ Grips, stocks, furniture ┤
                                                                             │
Spring steel ──→ Spring fabrication ──→ Trigger assemblies ──────────────────┤
                                                                             │
Copper alloys ──→ Brass fittings (sights, muzzle devices) ────────────────┘

Ammunition supply chain (see Section E) ──→ Loaded cartridges
Textiles ──→ Webbing/slings ──→ Rifle slings/straps
Rubber ──→ Grip/pad material
```

**Key bottlenecks:**
- **Stamping/forging presses:** High-tonnage presses for receiver blanks are precision tools; limited capacity.
- **Barrel rifling & drilling:** Precision boring mills; slow throughput.
- **Ammunition:** Primary constraint; small-arms production itself is usually fast relative to the ammunition it consumes.
- **Polymer/furniture:** Labor-intensive injection molding and hand assembly.

**Historical lead time:** WWII rifle production ~8–15 days from stock to finished rifle. [from training, unverified]

## 3. Industrial Mobilization: Converting Civilian to Military Production

The game's core premise is peacetime industry converting to war industry. Real-world mobilization patterns:

### The Timeline Problem
- **Months 0–6:** Retooling factories, retraining workers, shifting supply chains. Little new output (civilian goods still dominating).
- **Months 6–18:** Ramping production; raw material bottlenecks emerging. Output rising but not yet at peak.
- **Months 18–36:** Full mobilization plateau reached (if supply chain holds).
- **Year 4+:** Sustained war production; innovation and adaptation (new alloys, processes, supply substitutions).

WWII U.S. production curves: Aircraft output rose from 6,000 in 1940 to 96,000 in 1944—a 16x increase over 4 years, not overnight.

### What Converts Easily (Fast Ramp)
- **Automobile factories → Tank/vehicle assembly:** Ford, GM, Chrysler converted to tank/half-track production within 6–12 months.
- **Small-arms manufacturing:** Established civilian firearms plants (Winchester, Remington, etc.) ramped to military in 3–6 months.
- **Ammunition filling plants:** Labor-intensive, so retrain workers fast; output scales with workforce.

### What Converts Slowly (Hard Constraint)
- **Machine tools:** Precision machine tools (gun-boring mills, gear grinders, presses) cannot be improvised. New tool production takes 12–36 months; scarcity persists through the war.
- **Engines (especially aircraft/naval diesels):** Specialized foundries and machine shops; lead time 12–24 months for new capacity.
- **Explosives/ammonia plants:** Haber-Bosch process plants require 2–5 years to build; none can be rapidly expanded.
- **Shipyard & drydock expansion:** Impossible in wartime; existing capacity is the ceiling.
- **Raw material refining (smelters, refineries):** Bauxite alumina plants, copper smelters, titanium facilities: all 2–5 years to build new capacity.

### Critical Conversion Bottlenecks
1. **Skilled labor:** Machinists, welders, foundry workers cannot be trained in weeks. Shortages persist for years.
2. **Raw materials:** Competing demands (military vs. civilian) for copper, aluminum, nickel; rationing and substitution necessary.
3. **Machine tools:** The shortage of precision machine tools during WWII was described as the "invisible bottleneck" that limited all other production.
4. **Transportation & logistics:** Rail capacity, shipping, port facilities for raw materials become saturated.
5. **Energy:** Mobilization requires huge power increases; electrical grid expansion is slow.

### Government Mechanisms (Historical Reference)
- **War Production Boards:** U.S. (1942), Germany (Armaments Ministry), UK (Ministry of Supply). These agencies:
  - Prioritized contracts (military got first access to materials)
  - Allocated raw materials by plan
  - Regulated civilian production (often prohibited)
  - Coordinated retooling efforts
- **Substitution:** When materials were scarce, designs were altered (e.g., aluminum alloys vs. magnesium, steel vs. aluminum in airframes).
- **Geographic dispersal:** Factories were moved inland (away from bombing) and spread to distribute risk.

### Strategic Lessons for Game Design
- Mobilization is slow at first; output curves are exponential, not linear.
- Civilian goods production should be an option that players can sacrifice for military output (trade-off).
- Machine tools, explosives, and specific alloys should be hard-to-scale-quickly items.
- Finished products take time even if materials are available (assembly labor, testing, logistics).

## 4. Compact Recipe Suggestions for Game Implementation

Below are plausible input recipes for the game's production categories. These are *suggestions* drawn from the real-world chains above; the designer may adapt, simplify, or ignore them based on gameplay balance.

### Suggested Factory Input Recipes

| **Product** | **Prerequisite Building** | **Resource Inputs (per unit)** | **Build Time (game units)** | **Notes** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Steel** | Steel Mill | Iron ore (2), Coke (1) | 1 | Base metal; mass production |
| **Armor Steel** | Steel Mill (upgraded) | Steel (3), Chromium ore (1) | 2 | Superior armor; requires chromium deposit |
| **Advanced Armor** | Advanced Foundry | Armor Steel (3), Tungsten ore (1) | 3 | Tungsten is scarce; makes production expensive |
| **Aluminum Ingot** | Aluminum Smelter | Bauxite (2), Electricity (1) | 2 | Energy-intensive; consider power plant grid |
| **Titanium Ingot** | Titanium Refinery | Titanium ore (2), Coke (1), Electricity (1) | 3 | Complex process; geopolitically concentrated |
| **Copper Wire** | Wire Mill | Copper ore (1) | 1 | Common; supports electrical/ammunition chains |
| **Diesel Engine** | Engine Foundry | Steel (2), Copper (1), Oil (1) | 4 | Complex assembly; paces vehicle production |
| **Jet Engine** | Advanced Engine Works | Titanium (2), Nickel (1), Copper (1), Electricity (1) | 6 | Bottleneck for fighter aircraft |
| **Ammunition Case** | Ammunition Plant | Copper ore (2) or Steel (1) | 2 | High scrap rate; consider yielding 2–3 cases per input |
| **Explosives** | Chemical Plant | Ammonia (1), Nitrates (1), Oil (1) | 3 | Haber-Bosch dependency; Ammo ramp is gated here |
| **Loaded Ammunition** | Ammunition Plant | Ammunition Case (1), Explosives (1), Gunpowder (1) | 2 | Final product; requires all three prior inputs |
| **Rubber** | Rubber Plant | Oil (2) or Rubber plantation | 2 | Synthetic rubber from oil; or natural from plantation |
| **Firearms** | Small Arms Factory | Steel (1), Wood (1), Copper (1) | 2 | Labor-intensive; consider workforce requirement |
| **Artillery Gun** | Gun Foundry | Steel (3), Copper (1), Aluminum (1), Machine Tool (1 use) | 5 | Machine tools are consumed (represent expertise/tooling loss) |
| **Tank** | Tank Assembly Plant | Armor Steel (3), Diesel Engine (1), Copper (1), Rubber (1), Machine Tool (1 use) | 6 | Heavy vehicle; consumes machine tools |
| **Fighter Aircraft** | Aircraft Factory | Aluminum Ingot (2), Titanium Ingot (1), Jet Engine (1), Copper (1), Electronics (1) | 8 | High-tech; limited by jet engines and rare materials |
| **Warship (Frigate)** | Shipyard | Armor Steel (4), Diesel Engine (2), Copper (2), Rubber (1), Electronics (1) | 12 | Slow; tied to shipyard capacity (limit one per shipyard) |
| **Warship (Destroyer)** | Shipyard | Armor Steel (6), Diesel Engine (2), Copper (2), Torpedo components (1) | 16 | Larger; higher material costs |

### Optional Complexity: Machine Tools as Consumption Item

If machine tools are modeled as a strategic resource:
- Add a **Machine Tool Factory** that produces tools (inputs: steel, precision materials, electricity, skilled labor)
- Artillery, tanks, and complex firearms "consume" one machine tool per production (representing specialized tooling wear/replacement)
- Warfare can target machine-tool factories or interrupt supply
- Limits effective production scaling (a key historical constraint)

### Optional Complexity: Energy Grid

If electricity/fuel is tracked:
- Aluminum smelters, titanium refineries, aircraft/engine factories consume high electricity per unit
- Diesel engines, jet engines, and ships consume fuel to operate (military logistics cost)
- Power plants and oil refineries become strategic targets
- Mobilization increases total energy demand (bottleneck for scaling)

## 5. Gaps & Uncertainties

The following areas were harder to ground in concrete detail and may need designer interpretation:

1. **Component-level engineering:** Real production chains involve hundreds of sub-components (gearboxes, transmissions, hydraulic systems). The document summarizes major categories but doesn't decompose fully.

2. **Labor as a resource:** Real-world bottleneck but rarely modeled in strategy games. Should the game track "skilled worker pools"? Or abstracted into build times?

3. **Scrap, defects, and rework:** Military production has 5–20% reject rates depending on product. Does the game model waste, or assume 100% efficiency?

4. **Strategic stockpiles:** Nations maintain peacetime reserves of critical materials (e.g., U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve). Should the game include a "national strategic reserve" mechanic?

5. **Substitution under scarcity:** Real militaries adapted designs when materials ran short (aluminum → steel, etc.). Does the game allow design variants, or is substitution outside scope?

6. **Geographic dispersal & bombing:** WWII saw factories dispersed inland and underground. Does the game include this defensive production mechanic?
