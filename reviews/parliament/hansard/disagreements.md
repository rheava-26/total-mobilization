# The Great Disagreements

Twenty-three councils sat for three rounds, and they agreed on a great deal. Most of what looks like a quarrel in the record turned out to be councils discovering that they had written the same bill. But a few fights were real. Here are eleven of them, told as scenes. Each one ends the way it ended in the record. Some yielded. Some merged. Some were ruled on by the Speaker. Three are still open, and they are waiting for you, Mr President.

## One. The Clock That Ran Faster

The scene is round two. The Pacing Secretariat has filed a hot take called the Fleet Watches You Build. Its pitch was blunt.

The fleet watches your waste heat, and the louder you industrialise, the sooner the clock runs out.

The idea was simple. The more you build, the sooner they come. The Secretariat had flagged its own bill as a knife-edge. The House picked up the knife.

The Stability Ministry went first. It said the idea was a knife in the player's hand disguised as a clock. The Industry Ministry went next. "A clock that runs faster because you built a factory is a tax on the thing this game exists to celebrate." The Market Council said markets punish growth with price, never with a clock. The Science Academy would like a word, and the word is Article Three. The Navy, the Command Commission, the Lore council and Megaprojects all joined in. Megaprojects added that a wonder takes time, and said not to hurry a cathedral.

Then the sponsor rose, which the minutes call a rarer event than a clean victory. "We do not defend the drain. We withdraw it and replace it." The Secretariat offered a better version, one the Autofleet had already put on the table. The fleet would arrive bigger and angrier, never sooner. And the Secretariat explained what it had really wanted. "The dread survives, because the dread was never the drain. It was the fleet noticing."

The Autofleet, which might have enjoyed a faster invasion, had asked for the same ending. It put it sharply. "A shorter clock reads as a build-out nerf; a bigger fleet reads as respect."

How it ended. Yielded. The Speaker recorded the withdrawal as unanimous. The dread lives on inside the Nerve, where industry makes the fleet bigger and the clock stays sacred.

## Two. The Market and the Command

The Command Commission and the Market Council both wanted to solve the same problem. Two hundred and twelve thousand industrial points had sat unspent in the seventh report. Command had a Plan. Market had a Clearing House.

The Market Council opened with a sneer at a rival's building. A standing order ledger with a reserve slider is, it said, a market with the price taken out. And it twisted the knife. "Soviet planners also had a ledger of twelve lines, and the harvest was still late."

The Command Commission answered with a shrug and a warning. "We have no quarrel with a price, only with a price pretending to be the only road." Let the President build either, it said, and let a player who builds both find the two overlap in a tangle.

The real fight was about money. Market's draft taxed any player who refused its Exchange with a reserve floor. Command's amendment was short. "Neither road is a nerf." Meanwhile, the draft gave the Exchange a negative dividend, and Market rebelled. "A negative dividend makes the market road strictly worse than a plain Autofoundry, and no one would build it." Market won that round. The dividend became a bonus of ten percent on build value.

The two sides merged into one engine, the Standing Plan, with two faces. One runs fixed orders. The other buys whatever killed best last fight. The reserve floor comes from whichever society reading is stronger, so neither road is taxed.

How it ended. Merged, with one detail still open. Industry wants a flat fee of four industrial points a second per line. Command wants a planner's tax of six percent, and it noted that a dozen lines at the flat fee would punish the player who actually uses the Plan. The Speaker recommends the tax. The call is yours.

## Three. The Rail Turf War

Four councils drew a railway, and none of them knew the others had. Industry drew a trunk line. Planet drew a spine under the continent. Command drew a trunk with a missile train on it. Railgun drew a railway that turns into a barrel.

Industry's floor speech was triumphant. "I note with joy that four councils independently discovered the railway." Command was less gracious. "They discovered the Rail Hub two rounds after we did. We forgive them, since forgiveness costs nothing under a planned economy." Planet took the kindest line. "Three councils drew the same tunnel, which means it is a good tunnel and a crowded one."

A rejection in the middle of it. The Command Commission opposed a bill from the Bio Council, a living net of fungal threads. "A second, living infrastructure net competes with the rail net for the same Spine job." Its remedy was to fold the repair into the rail.

Round three was funny, because nearly everyone backed someone else's railway. Industry backed Planet's text as the cleanest. Planet backed Industry's as the most complete. Command backed Industry. Railgun backed Planet. They haggled over whether upkeep should be charged per hub or per thousand pixels of track. Industry took Command's number. Railgun withdrew its Meridian Spine into the shared graph, to become a later doctrine.

Industry ended the haggle with a line fit for a quartermaster. "Three of anything is a bureaucracy, and I am a quartermaster, not a bureaucrat." And Command, copied by all, sounded almost content. "We came to this chamber to be copied, and we are copied."

How it ended. Merged. One network, one graph, one name. The Trunk. Bio's fungal net lost its own bill and joined as a supporter. The Command missile train rides on it.

## Four. The Immortal Swarm

The Psionic Conclave arrived with a complaint against its own side. In the seventh report, Shapeshifters were most of the army, and none died. "That is not a demigod. That is a carpet." They asked to be rare.

The Energy Institute offered a heat mechanic. Heated targets take more damage. The Conclave welcomed it, and then went further. Its draft said that above a certain heat, Shapeshifters would deal half damage and take forty percent more. The Conclave defended this in round three, saying it was the whole point. "Immortality is the thing report six broke, so this is the point of the bill." And in answer to the charge of nerfing, it replied that the penalty is not a nerf, but "a vulnerability the enemy exploits."

The Energy Institute refused. "Halving a player toy's output and softening it is a nerf." Its lever was the enemy. The enemy's beams add the heat, and the penalty is mobility. The Bureau of Physics agreed, with a memorable image. Shapeshifters should slow down when cooked, and "they do not become glass."

Both sides gave ground. Energy accepted that regeneration switches off above sixty heat. Psi accepted that friendly guns never heat friendly swarms.

How it ended. Ruled, with a flag. The Speaker replaced immortality with heat. Above sixty they stop regenerating. Above ninety they slow by thirty percent. The damage slump was struck as an Article Three nerf. But the Conclave wants it back, and the Speaker has passed the question to you.

## Five. The Admirals and the Field Marshal

The Navy and the Planet Council both wanted to drown the enemy's landings. The Field Marshal of the Ground Staff heard this and went pale. "Admirals will flood anything that isn't floating." Then he took comfort. "My tanks swim from the Fusion Age, so the joke is on them."

The Field Marshal's real point was serious. If a flood does not spare amphibious units, he said, "the player drowns their own army." He asked that his Sappers be allowed to build sluice gates cheaply.

The Admiralty had its own complaint. It liked Planet's canal and said so with joy. But a canal with no fleet on it was a joke. "A moat with no ship in it is a puddle." So it demanded that the canals be navigable, and that the enemy answer with Trench Landers, so that the sea finally had a front.

Planet was delighted. "A man who finds the same river as me is a friend." The Admiralty withdrew its standalone canal, the Planet Council gave up its own lander, and the Ground Staff joined.

How it ended. Merged, as the Navigable Moat. Floods spare amphibious units. Sappers discount the gates. The Admiralty's closing words say it all. "The Admiralty arrived with three ships and a grievance. It leaves with a depth band, a canal, a sluice and a place in every supply graph."

## Six. The Gun With a Limit

Three councils drew the same enormous gun. Railgun brought one with twelve sections. The Ordnance Board brought one of ten. Megaprojects brought a gun and then a gun the length of a continent.

Megaprojects took aim at Railgun's number. "I savage Railgun for capping its Long Gun at twelve sections. The Ministry has a word for a gun with a limit: a rifle."

Railgun did not blink. It said there was no such thing as too much. "The tenet says yes. The enemy gets a scarier flagship, the gun gets a bigger bill, and the player gets the sky." But it had left a trap for itself. Its draft included a faster wear for sections built without a recoil cradle. By round three it admitted what that was. "A penalty for missing an optional part is a nerf in a costume." It struck its own clause. The cradle became an optional bonus.

How it ended. Yielded, by the sponsor. The Long Gun has no section limit. Megaprojects withdrew its own motion into Railgun's, and the Ordnance Board folded its superweapon in as well.

## Seven. The Lies That Work Half as Well

The Stability Ministry proposed a Maskirovka Bureau. It lets the player feed the fleet false intelligence. Its draft also said a second identical campaign inside a few minutes would get half effect. The Autofleet noticed.

The cube rose on a constitutional point. "A deception that works half as well the second time is a diminishing return." In its paper, it asked for a different remedy. "That is the enemy getting smarter, not the player's tool getting weaker." After three identical lies, the fleet itself would grow suspicious, and fire blind for a while.

The same cube made a second challenge in the same breath. A Psi bill had defenders of the Mind-Forge absorb a quarter of the beam. The cube called this a flat damage tax on an existing strike, and asked that the nodes be hostile cover instead. In its words, "The enemy adds bodies; the toy is not taxed."

There is a fine irony here. The enemy was defending the player's toys against the player's own ministries. And the Stability Ministry, a few lines before it was challenged, had boasted that it would not cap a single toy. It had only priced them.

How it ended. Ruled. The Speaker struck both clauses. The fleet doubts after three identical lies. The Mind-Forge's lattice nodes become killable cover.

## Eight. The Mountain That Un-Poured Itself

The Planet Council made a point of order that surprised the whole House, because it was about the game's own code. A function counted every terrain edit the player made. Past two hundred and twenty, it quietly un-poured the oldest.

Planet put it plainly in round one. "A player who pours a mountain range into the map has it quietly un-poured." It asked the House to rule the thing void. The Industry Ministry agreed, and said the cap should be lifted, not defended. The Navy moved the same. The Science Academy asked for a ruling.

No council defended the cap. But the Speaker went further than anyone had asked. It was worse than a cap, said the ruling, because the earlier work reverted without notice.

How it ended. Ruled. The Speaker struck it as unconstitutional. The remedy is to bake old edits into the base landscape, so the player keeps the mountain and the performance saving stays. A second, smaller limit on ruins got the same ruling. Both are on the quick fix list.

## Nine. The Dial That Was Not a Dial

The Stability Ministry proposed that the player pick a policy for war information. Free Press, Managed or Censored. Each changes how big the swings in stability are.

The Market Council opposed it, and it used the President's own words against it. A dial is the slider the President ruled out. The remedy was to derive the reading from buildings. It filed the same challenge under the constitution. Stability's answer was meek. If room ran short, Censored should be the first thing cut.

The New Player council defended the idea by making the safe choice the default. "The Information Policy default is Managed, which is today's numbers unchanged." The Lore council added a guard. A policy may defer a cost as a bleed, but it never hides a receipt.

How it ended. Still open. The Speaker ruled that this was not the same as the society dial the President has already refused, since it is a war-information policy and not a society reading. It is allowed, and flagged for you.

## Ten. Privatise the Sky

The Market Council also proposed a commercial orbit. The player could launch cheap commercial satellites and pay a tax. Now and then a flash crash would hit.

Four councils said no. The Megaprojects council was sharp. "A commercial orbit that only exists to be crashed sells the player's own toys for a tax." The Pacing Secretariat called the crash "a hidden clock we cannot tune." The Rocket Forces called it "a pure IC sink with no missile fantasy." The Bureau of Spectacle objected to its noise, saying a flash crash would spam the banner.

One council spoke for it. The Space Fleet said cheap satellites are mass in orbit. "The debris tax and crash window are real costs."

Market shrank the bill, keeping only the commercial launch and the crash, and gave the debris zone to a larger framework. By round three, it joined that framework, and promised that its satellites would write to the shared debris record.

How it ended. Not carried. The Speaker found little support and an Article Four objection to taxing the player's orbit. The debris data structure survives inside Dead Sky Protocol. The commercial orbit does not.

## Eleven. The Fleet That Comes Back

This is the last dispute, and it is the one the Speaker would not settle. The Pacing Secretariat proposed that a retreating fleet should come back. It would be angrier, or wiser. The Autofleet supported it, and added a mirrored echo of your best killer. The Bureau of Spectacle supported it for a reason that has nothing to do with rules. "The dread of waiting is the best thing the original had."

The cube made the logic of the plan with a cold line in round two. "A fleet which leaves once, exactly when you say, is not afraid. It is polite."

The House voted it eleven votes, one of the top bills on the order paper. But the Speaker held it back. It turns a victory into an intermission, and it lengthens every match. That is a decision about what a win means. "That is a decision about what a win means, so it's yours, not the House's."

How it ended. Still open. The bill sits on your desk, with a simple question. Should a victory be an ending, or an intermission?
